# Spaced Repetition for Motor Learning

*A follow-up companion to `motorLearningForDance.md`: does "practise often, at the edge" (get good fast) really contradict "practise again just as you start to forget" (keep it for life), and what does the motor-learning literature say about the gap between sessions for a dance figure?*

---

## Scope & method

Same rules as the parent brief (`motorLearningForDance.md`, "Scope & method"): every load-bearing claim traces to the paper that owns it, read via the journal page, PubMed/PMC abstract, the author's PDF, or the full text where I could get it. Blog and app-marketing summaries were used only as pointers. This file **does not repeat** the parent brief; it cites it by section (e.g. "parent §3") where a finding is already covered there.

**Evidence labels used inline:** **PRIMARY** · **RCT-BACKED** · **META-BACKED** · **THEORY-DERIVED** · **CONTESTED** · **SPECULATIVE** · **(unverified)** — same meanings as the parent brief.

**What was verified how.** Full text read: Donovan & Radosevich 1999; Lee & Genovese 1988; Cepeda et al. 2006 and 2008; Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021; Tatel & Ackerman 2025 (accepted-manuscript PDF); SM-2 spec (SuperMemo site); FSRS algorithm wiki. Abstract read on PubMed / publisher / Semantic Scholar: everything else with a PMID or DOI in the Sources. **Marked (unverified):** Arthur et al. 1998's *per-category* effect sizes (the abstract figures are verified; the moderator table came through OCR garbled, so I report only what the abstract states); Leitner 1972 (secondary description only); the Schmidt & Lee textbook pointer; the Rickard 2008 paper the brief's lead called "Spacing and the explicit–implicit distinction" — **no paper with that title exists**; the correct references are Rickard, Cai, Rieth, Jones & Ard (2008) "Sleep does not enhance motor sequence learning" and Rieth et al. (2010), used below.

**A blunt caveat.** The between-session spacing literature for *motor* skills is small, mostly surgical-simulator and lab-task work, with samples of 10–40 per arm. It agrees on the direction of the effect and is nearly silent on the *shape of the schedule* (expanding vs fixed, what interval, how to handle a lapse). Everything in §6–§7 about schedules is therefore extrapolated from verbal-memory research and labelled as such. Where the evidence for the app's actual design question is thin, this brief says so rather than filling the gap with confidence.

## TL;DR — ranked by leverage for the app

1. **The contradiction is mostly a confusion between *frequency* and *massing*.** "Practise often" is compatible with spacing: daily sessions on consecutive days *are* distributed practice, and the surgical-simulator literature finds consecutive-day spacing at least as good as, often better than, weekly spacing and far better than a single cram. What hurts is cramming all reps into one block, not practising every day. *(Donovan & Radosevich 1999; Shea et al. 2000; Fahl et al. 2023; Verdaasdonk et al. 2007 — META/RCT-BACKED.)* See §0.

2. **Between-session spacing does not cost acquisition speed for motor skills — in the studies that measured it, it *helped* acquisition too.** Spaced groups performed better during the remaining practice sessions (Shea et al. 2000), during acquisition (Dail & Christina 2004), and matched massed groups immediately post-training then beat them at retention (Moulton et al. 2006). Hypothesis A's premise ("massed gets you good faster") is not supported for sessions spread across days. **RCT-BACKED**, small samples.

3. **The minimum useful gap is one night's sleep.** Re-practising a sequence within the same day recovers performance but adds little learning (reactive inhibition / fatigue account); a 24 h gap with sleep produces the between-session gains the sleep literature reports. A gap of ~4–6 h also matters if a *different, similar* skill is practised in between (consolidation interference) — but that finding is CONTESTED. *(Rickard et al. 2008; Pan & Rickard 2015; Simmons 2012; Kwon et al. 2015; Brashers-Krug et al. 1996 vs Caithness et al. 2004.)* See §4.

4. **Nobody has shown that *expanding* intervals beat *fixed* intervals for a motor skill; for verbal memory the meta-analytic difference is ~zero (g = 0.03).** What matters is total spacing and number of separate sessions, not the growth pattern. The SM-2/FSRS "expand on success" logic is therefore an engineering convenience, not a motor-learning finding. *(Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011; Latimier et al. 2021; Walsh et al. 2023 — META-BACKED, verbal; motor: no direct test found.)* See §2.

5. **Motor skills decay slowly and unevenly — and continuous, whole-body skills barely decay at all.** The newest meta-analysis (1,344 effects) estimates ~0.06–0.08 SD lost per month of non-use, with half of acquisition gains gone at ~6.5 months (accuracy) to ~13 months (speed). Discrete, sequence-like, closed-loop tasks decay fastest; continuous, natural, physical tasks slowest. A dance *figure* (a discrete sequence) is on the fast-decay side; *dancing* (continuous, natural) is on the slow side. *(Tatel & Ackerman 2025; Arthur et al. 1998 — META-BACKED.)* See §3.

6. **Retrieval beats re-exposure for motor sequences, including dance figures — and re-watching the demo is re-exposure.** Testing at the end of a skills course produced d ≈ 0.9 at two weeks over equal-time practice; selectively *retrieving* dance figures strengthened them (and induced forgetting of related, un-retrieved figures), whereas *restudy* did not. So "do it from memory, then check" is the right unit of practice, and it dovetails with the parent brief's error-estimation protocol (parent §2). *(Kromann et al. 2009; Tempel, Loran & Frings 2015 — RCT-BACKED.)* See §5.

7. **The verbal heuristic "optimal gap ≈ 10–20 % of the retention interval" is real but verbal-only.** Its motor analog is directionally supported (longer target retention → longer gaps tolerated) but unquantified. Use it as a sanity check on interval caps, not as a formula. *(Cepeda et al. 2008; Rohrer & Pashler 2007 — META/RCT-BACKED for facts; SPECULATIVE for figures.)* See §1c.

8. **A single "best interval" does not exist; the two published attempts to find one for a motor skill landed on "2 days is better than 1 week" (arthroscopy) and "1–2 days ≈ 6–8 days" (laparoscopy).** Weekly vs monthly also tied for vascular anastomosis. Read together: for a skill in active acquisition, anything from every other day to weekly works, provided sleep intervenes; beyond a week, evidence thins fast. *(Yang et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2023; Mitchell et al. 2011 — RCT-BACKED, n ≈ 10–13 per arm.)* See §2b.

## 0. The apparent contradiction, resolved

**Hypothesis A ("get good fast"): practise it often, always at the edge of ability — massed / high-frequency, challenge-point.**
**Hypothesis B ("keep it for life"): leave time between sessions, ideally re-practising just as forgetting starts — spaced repetition with expanding intervals.**

The literature resolves this in four moves, and the resolution is *not* quite the tidy "both true for different phases" shape one might expect.

**(i) Hypothesis A bundles three different things, and only one of them is supported.** "Often" (high session *frequency*), "massed" (many reps in one block with no rest), and "at the edge" (challenge point) are three orthogonal dials. The challenge-point dial (parent §4) governs difficulty *within* a session and is untouched by anything below. The *frequency* dial is not in tension with spacing at all: practising a figure every day on consecutive days is a spaced schedule, and the surgical-simulator evidence finds consecutive-day spacing the best-performing interval it has tested (Fahl et al. 2023 review; Verdaasdonk 2007; Spruit 2015/2017; Yang 2023). Only the *massing* dial — cramming the week's reps into one long block — is actually opposed to spacing, and there the evidence is against Hypothesis A even on its own terms: massed practice depresses performance during acquisition (Lee & Genovese 1988: d ≈ 0.96 in favour of distributed *at end of acquisition*), and between-session massing did not produce faster acquisition in any of the motor studies that measured it (§1b). **So Hypothesis A's premise — that massing buys speed — fails for motor skills.** What "practise often" *does* buy is more total sessions per calendar week, and number of sessions is precisely the variable that best predicts retention (Walsh et al. 2023, verbal; Baddeley & Longman 1978, typing). **META/RCT-BACKED.**

**(ii) Hypothesis B is right about spacing and wrong — or at least unsupported — about the schedule shape.** Distributed practice across days improves motor retention (§1b), and there is a defensible verbal-memory mechanism ("reminding" — Benjamin & Tullis 2010) for why a gap that is *as long as retrieval still succeeds* is best (§5). But the second half of Hypothesis B, *expanding* intervals à la SM-2/FSRS, has no motor evidence and only null-to-tiny verbal evidence once absolute spacing is equated (Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011: "absolute spacing enhances learning regardless of relative spacing"; Latimier et al. 2021: expanding vs uniform g = 0.034). **META-BACKED (verbal).** The "re-practise just as forgetting starts" picture also assumes a steep forgetting curve. Motor skills forget slowly (§3): ~0.06–0.08 SD per month, and continuous skills barely at all. A schedule tuned to catch a fact before it vanishes in days is tuned to the wrong time-constant for a figure that loses half its gains in six to thirteen months.

**(iii) The costs are asymmetric, which is why the "contradiction" feels sharp in practice but isn't.** Spacing a figure across days costs essentially nothing in acquisition (§1b) and buys retention. Massing costs retention and buys — at best — the *appearance* of skill during the session (parent §4, Schmidt & Bjork 1992 / Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). The one real trade-off is *calendar* speed: if you only have two sessions this week, spacing them Mon/Thu beats Mon/Mon, but four sessions Mon–Thu beat both. There is no evidence that any spacing pattern lets you learn a figure in fewer *sessions* than another; there is good evidence that more sessions, each separated by sleep, beat fewer longer ones.

**(iv) Where the honest shape *does* differ by phase.** The consolidation literature (§4) gives a phase-dependent *minimum* gap: while a figure is being acquired, the next session should be after a night's sleep, and practising a *similar competing* figure within a few hours *may* interfere (CONTESTED). After acquisition, the decay literature (§3) gives a phase-dependent *maximum* gap: a figure that has been overlearned and is embedded in continuous dancing tolerates weeks to months; a freshly learned discrete sequence does not. In between, the evidence supports **fixed or loosely expanding gaps of roughly 1–7 days during acquisition, then longer fixed gaps with a cap for maintenance** — but the *expanding* part is a reasonable engineering default rather than a finding.

**Net:** both hypotheses survive, but as (A′) "practise in many short sessions, each at the challenge point, with sleep between" and (B′) "once it's in, keep re-practising it *from memory* at gaps that stay inside the retrieval-success window; the exact growth law of the gaps is not something the literature can tell you." The dial that Hypothesis A cares about (difficulty within a session) and the dial that Hypothesis B cares about (gap between sessions) are independent. Sections 1–6 give the evidence; §7 turns it into app design.

## 1. Does spacing work for motor skills, and how big is the effect?

**Finding.** Yes, robustly — but "spacing" is two different literatures with different effect sizes. *Within-session* distribution (rest between trials, seconds to minutes) has large, well-replicated effects that are partly *performance* effects (fatigue) and that reverse for some discrete tasks. *Between-session* distribution (days) has smaller-sample but consistent evidence for better retention and transfer, and no acquisition cost. Effects shrink sharply as task complexity rises.

### 1a. Within-session (inter-trial) distribution — the classic "massed vs distributed" literature

- **Lee & Genovese (1988),** *Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport* 59(4):277–287 — **VERIFIED (full text).** Meta-analysis of 47 articles / 52 effect sizes, distribution defined by inter-trial interval. At end of acquisition, distributed > massed with a weighted mean ES of **0.96**; on **absolute retention** (the only measure they argue reflects learning rather than temporary performance), distributed > massed with ES **≈ 0.49–0.53**. Their conclusion: massed practice depresses *both* performance and learning. **META-BACKED / PRIMARY.**
- **Lee & Genovese (1989),** *RQES* 60(1):59–65 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Same task made discrete (one timing estimate per trial) vs continuous (twenty per trial), 25 s vs 0.5 s inter-trial intervals. **Distributed helped the continuous version; *massed* helped the discrete version**, in both acquisition and retention. **RCT-BACKED.** This is the origin of the textbook caution that inter-trial spacing effects depend on task type.
- **Donovan & Radosevich (1999),** *Journal of Applied Psychology* 84(5):795–805 — **VERIFIED (full text).** 63 studies, 112 effect sizes, overall **d = 0.46** (95 % CI 0.42–0.50) favouring spaced. Task-cluster moderator: **simple, physical, low-mental tasks d = 0.97**; high-mental/average-complexity d = 0.42; **high-complexity clusters d = 0.11 and 0.07** (not significantly different from each other). Overall task complexity correlated **r = −0.25** with effect size; mental and physical load per se did not. Acquisition (d = 0.45) vs retention (d = 0.51) did not differ — but note their "retention" was anything ≥ 24 h, so this is not long-term retention. Studies of low methodological rigor had d = 1.22 vs 0.40 for higher rigor. Their intertrial-interval categories: < 1 min d = 0.71; 1–10 min 0.48; 10 min–1 h 0.26; **> 1 day 0.16** — they explicitly warn this is confounded with task type and does not mean "shorter is better"; different tasks have different optimal intervals. **META-BACKED.**
  - **Coaching-relevant reading:** for a *complex* skill like a full figure with a partner, the within-session rest-interval lever is weak (d ≈ 0.1). Within-session structure is the companion file's territory (`withinSessionPracticeStructure.md`); this brief only notes that the *big* classic effect sizes come from simple tasks and should not be quoted for dance.
- **Panchuk, Spittle, Johnston & Spittle (2013),** *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 116(3):750–760 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Australian-football handball (a discrete sport skill), 1 s vs 30 s between reps: accuracy improved in the **massed** condition from pre-test to 10-min and 2-week retention. Consistent with Lee & Genovese 1989 for discrete skills. **RCT-BACKED, small.**

### 1b. Between-session spacing (days) — the literature that matters for the app

- **Baddeley & Longman (1978),** *Ergonomics* 21(8):627–635 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 72 postal workers learning to type, 1 h or 2 h sessions, once or twice a day, to 60 total hours. **One 1-hour session per day was most efficient per hour of training; two 2-hour sessions per day least.** Retention at 1, 3 and 9 months showed ~30 % loss of speed. The classic demonstration that *more hours per day* is worse per hour — but note the 1 h/day group took the most calendar weeks; the paper is explicit that this is the operational trade-off. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **Shea, Lai, Black & Park (2000),** *Human Movement Science* 19:737–760 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Exp. 1: continuous dynamic-balance task, two sessions of 7 trials separated by **20 min vs 24 h**. Exp. 2: three key-press timing task variants, sessions within a day vs across days. Spacing across days "resulted in the enhancement of performance during the remaining practice sessions *and* enhanced learning as assessed by the delayed retention test." **RCT-BACKED.** The key sentence for Hypothesis A: spacing across days did not slow acquisition; it improved the next session.
- **Dail & Christina (2004),** *RQES* 75(2):148–155 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 90 novice golfers, 240 putts either all on one day or 60/day over four days. Distributed group performed better **during acquisition and at retention**; retention interval (1, 7, 28 days) made no difference to the group effect. Distributed learners also *predicted* better retention (metacognition tracked reality). **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Moulton, Dubrowski, MacRae, Graham, Grober & Reznick (2006),** *Annals of Surgery* 244(3):400–409 — **VERIFIED (abstract + ASA meeting abstract).** 38 junior surgical residents, microvascular anastomosis, same total time, **massed (1 day) vs distributed (weekly)**. Both improved immediately; at 1-month retention distributed was better on time (200 vs 244 s), hand movements (176 vs 235) and expert global rating (15 vs 12); on transfer to a live rat, distributed beat massed on every expert measure (global 15 vs 7; competency-for-OR 12 vs 2). **RCT-BACKED** — the strongest single motor spacing study, because the transfer test is a real task.
- **Spruit, Band & Hamming (2015),** *Surgical Endoscopy* 29(8):2235–2243 — **VERIFIED (title/venue; results via review pointers).** Three 75-min box-trainer sessions on one day vs one per week for three weeks; spaced superior at acquisition and retention. **RCT-BACKED.** **Spruit, Band, van der Heijden & Hamming (2017),** *J. Surgical Education* 74(3):530–538 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Four arms (n = 35–40 each): massed; massed with two 45-min breaks; breaks with 35-min naps; three consecutive days. At end of training *and* ~3-month retention: **spaced > break-nap > break > massed**; spaced and nap groups beat break and massed, **effect sizes 0.20–0.37**. So (a) breaks alone don't rescue massing, (b) sleep in the gap is what matters, (c) the effect for a complex skill is small-to-moderate, not the d ≈ 1 of the simple-task literature. **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Verdaasdonk, Stassen, van Wijk & Dankelman (2007),** *Surgical Endoscopy* 21(2):214–219 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** VR endoscopy, distributed *within* one day vs over three consecutive days (n = 10 each). At 7-day post-test, the across-days group was **18.7 % faster** with equal errors and path length. **RCT-BACKED, tiny.**
- **Andersen, Konge, Cayé-Thomasen & Sørensen (2016),** *JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery* 142(7):635–640 — **VERIFIED (full article page).** VR mastoidectomy, 12 procedures either in one day or in six blocks ≥ 3 days apart; retention at ~3 months. **Final-product scores were retained equally** (mean change 0.1, p = .89) — a null at the headline level. But the massed group improved 1.8 points *between* the first and second retention procedure (p = .001) and had to compensate by removing more bone in the last 10 min, i.e. their skill was less consolidated and needed a warm-up. **RCT-BACKED — a useful CONTESTED data point: the retention advantage of spacing can be invisible in a single-shot test and show up as "needs a warm-up rep."**
- **Kwon, Kwon & Lee (2015),** *J. Physical Therapy Science* 27(3):769–772 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Serial-reaction-time task, three sessions separated by **10 min vs 12 h (including sleep)**, n = 15 each. Reaction time improved more with the 12-h gaps (group × test interaction), accuracy did not differ. **RCT-BACKED, small; a cleaner version of the same point is Simmons 2012, §4.**
- **Krigolson, Ferguson, Colino & Binsted (2021),** *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 128(2):885–899 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Speed-stacking sequence across ten sessions, massed vs distributed, with observational learning. Retention favoured distributed; the *during-training* advantage of distributed appeared only in session one. **RCT-BACKED.** Consistent with "spacing helps retention, not the look of practice."
- **Reviews:** **Cecilio-Fernandes, Cnossen, Jaarsma & Tio (2018),** *J. Surgical Education* 75(2):471–480 — **VERIFIED (title/venue).** Systematic review of RCTs of spacing in surgical motor skills; concludes intervals between practices matter and deserve study in their own right. **Fahl et al. (2023),** *BMC Medical Education* — **VERIFIED (PMC full text).** Seven studies of spacing in VR simulator psychomotor training; 5/7 favoured spaced; "spacing across consecutive days seemed more effective than shorter or longer spacing intervals"; too heterogeneous for meta-analysis; explicitly proposes an inverted-U (long enough to shed fatigue, short enough not to lose the skill) and calls for larger trials. **Yeung et al. (2020),** *Resuscitation* 156:61–71 (ILCOR) — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 17 studies of spaced vs massed resuscitation training; 15/17 favoured spaced at up to 1 year; **certainty of evidence very low** (serious risk of bias), no meta-analysis possible. **META-BACKED (weakly).**
- **Anderson, Sebaldt, Lin & Cheng (2019),** *Resuscitation* 135:153–161 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 167 participants randomised to CPR refreshers **monthly, 3-monthly, 6-monthly or yearly**, each refresher = brief practice to "excellent CPR" with feedback. At 12 months: **58 % excellent CPR in the monthly group vs 26 % / 21 % / 15 %.** The cleanest published dose-response for a *maintenance* interval on a physical skill: monthly beat quarterly by a wide margin. **RCT-BACKED.**

### 1c. The verbal-memory benchmark — say clearly it is verbal

- **Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006),** *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380 — **VERIFIED (full text).** 839 assessments from 317 experiments, **restricted by design to verbal recall tasks.** Optimal inter-study interval (ISI) increases with retention interval; at retention intervals over a month, the optimal ISI is longer than a day. **META-BACKED (verbal).** *(One WebFetch summary claimed this meta-analysis "also covers procedural learning" — it does not; the paper states "we restricted our analysis to verbal memory tasks.")*
- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008),** *Psychological Science* 19(11):1095–1102 — **VERIFIED (full text).** > 1,350 people, trivia facts, gaps up to 105 days, retention intervals 7–350 days. Optimal gap **≈ 20 % of the retention interval for delays of a few weeks, falling to ≈ 5–10 % at a year**: optimal recall gaps were 1, 11, 21, 21 days for RIs of 7, 35, 70, 350 days. Too-short gaps cost far more than too-long ones (the "ridgeline" is asymmetric). **RCT-BACKED (verbal).** **Rohrer & Pashler (2007),** *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 16(4):183–186 — the "optimal ISI ≈ 10–20 % of RI" summary. **PRIMARY (verbal).**

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- **Effect size shrinks with complexity** (Donovan: d ≈ 0.1 for complex tasks; Spruit 2017: 0.2–0.4 for laparoscopy). Do not expect the d ≈ 1 of simple-task lab work in a dance studio.
- **Within-session and between-session are different mechanisms.** Within-session massed practice depresses performance partly through fatigue/reactive inhibition (§4); between-session spacing works through consolidation and retrieval (§4–§5). A within-session finding does not license a between-session rule, and vice versa.
- **Every between-session motor study above has n ≈ 10–40 per arm** and most are surgical-simulator tasks with fine-motor, tool-mediated demands. The transfer to whole-body partnered movement is **SPECULATIVE** in the specific numbers, though the direction is consistent across every task type tried.
- **Null results exist** (Andersen 2016 on the primary outcome; Mitchell 2011 weekly = monthly, §2b) and they cluster where the retention test is a single shot and the skill was trained to a proficiency criterion. Spacing's benefit may show up as "less warm-up needed" rather than "higher first-attempt score."

**Coaching / app translation.**
- Treat *sessions per week with sleep between* as the primary lever, not *reps per session*. Two figures each practised on four days beat the same figures practised for four times as long on one day, and the cost in acquisition is zero to negative.
- For a complex figure, do not agonise over inter-trial rest within a session on spacing grounds — the effect is small there (see the companion file for the within-session questions that *do* matter).
- Treat the "10–20 % of retention interval" rule as a *ceiling sanity check*: if you want a figure reliable at a competition 8 weeks out, a gap longer than ~10–14 days between practices is past what even the forgiving verbal ridgeline would recommend. **SPECULATIVE** in the numbers.

## 2. Expanding vs uniform / fixed intervals for motor skills

**Finding.** Expanding intervals are the *intuition* behind every flashcard algorithm, and the intuition is older than the evidence. For verbal memory, once total spacing is equated, expanding and uniform schedules are indistinguishable on average, with expanding winning only under specific conditions (low initial learning, high interference, no feedback, many retrievals). For motor skills, **I found no experiment that directly compares expanding with uniform between-session schedules.** The nearest motor evidence compares *different fixed* intervals and mostly finds ties.

### 2a. Verbal memory — where the idea comes from and where it stands

- **Landauer & Bjork (1978),** in *Practical Aspects of Memory* (Gruneberg, Morris & Sykes, eds.), Academic Press, pp. 625–632 — **VERIFIED (author PDF, Bjork lab).** Name learning; for *test*-type rehearsal an expanding pattern of intervals was best within a fixed session; for *re-presentation* rehearsal, uniform spacing was slightly better. Note both halves: even the origin paper found expanding superior only for retrieval, not restudy. **PRIMARY.**
- **Karpicke & Roediger (2007),** *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition* 33(4):704–719 — **VERIFIED (title/venue).** Text passages, recall tested 1 week later: **expanding promoted short-term retention; equally spaced retrieval enhanced long-term retention.** **RCT-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Karpicke & Bauernschmidt (2011),** *JEP:LMC* 37(5):1250–1257 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Repeated retrieval with long gaps gave a ~200 % improvement in long-term retention over no-gap retrieval; **no evidence that expanding, equal or contracting relative spacing was superior** once absolute spacing was equated. **RCT-BACKED (verbal).** Title says it: *absolute spacing enhances learning regardless of relative spacing.*
- **Storm, Bjork & Storm (2010),** *Memory & Cognition* 38(2):244–253 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Expanding beat uniform at one week **only when the material between retrievals was highly interfering**, i.e. when the memory was vulnerable to forgetting. **RCT-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Küpper-Tetzel, Kapler & Wiseheart (2014),** *Memory & Cognition* 42(5):729–741 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Three sessions over days: **contracting** best for retention ≤ 7 days; equal and expanding best at 35 days. **RCT-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Toppino, Phelan & Gerbier (2018),** *Memory & Cognition* 46(6):969–984 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Pseudoword pairs over 13 days: expanding superior after **low-level** initial training, no advantage after **high-level** initial training (five rounds of practice testing). Interpreted as study-phase retrieval: expanding helps when the first retrieval would otherwise fail. **RCT-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021),** *Educational Psychology Review* 33(3):959–987 — **VERIFIED (full text).** 29 studies. Spaced vs massed retrieval: **g = 0.74**. **Expanding vs uniform: g = 0.034, not significant**; the more times an item is retrieved, the more expanding helps. Authors: results "do not support the widely held belief that inter-retrieval intervals should be progressively increased." **META-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Walsh, Krusmark, Jastrembski, Hansen, Honn & Gunzelmann (2023),** *Memory & Cognition* 51(2):455–472 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Declarative items; retention depended strongly on the **number of sessions an item appeared in**, not on how repetitions were distributed among them. **RCT-BACKED (verbal).**
- **Cepeda et al. (2006)** on expanding vs fixed ISI: 22 comparisons, mostly paired associates; expanding nominally better, but "large standard errors … make conclusions … necessarily tentative"; three multi-day studies split one-each for expanding, fixed, and no difference; they float feedback presence as the unmeasured moderator. **META-BACKED, inconclusive.**

### 2b. Motor / procedural skills — what exists

- **Simmons (2012),** *Journal of Research in Music Education* 59(4):357–368 — **VERIFIED (ERIC abstract).** 29 non-pianist musicians, 9-note keyboard sequence, three sessions separated by **5 min, 6 h or 24 h**. Accuracy improved into session 2 **only with 24 h gaps**; speed improved into session 3 with **6 h and 24 h** gaps but not 5 min. Not an expanding-vs-fixed test, but the best musical evidence that a same-day second session is largely wasted for accuracy. **RCT-BACKED, small.**
- **Mitchell et al. (2011),** *American Journal of Surgery* 201(1):31–39 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 24 interns, end-to-side vascular anastomosis, **weekly for 4 weeks vs monthly for 4 months**, equal total time; no difference in acquisition or 4-month retention on any measure. **RCT-BACKED, tiny.** A *fixed* 7-day gap tied a *fixed* 30-day gap.
- **Tang et al. (2023),** *Surgical Endoscopy* (PMC10776690) — **VERIFIED (PMC full text).** 39 students, proficiency-based laparoscopy, **1–2 days vs 6–8 days between sessions**: time to proficiency ~291–299 min in both, retention (3–5 weeks) 94–96 min in both, p = .81/.91. Authors' reading: as long as a night's sleep separates sessions, interval length in this range does not matter. **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Yang et al. (2023),** *Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy* 31(12):5546–5553 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 30 residents, arthroscopy simulator, massed training to criterion then retraining at **1-day, 2-day or 1-week** intervals; retention at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months. **2-day beat 1-week** on diagnostic-arthroscopy time at post-test and at 3 months; 1-day ≈ 2-day; most other metrics null. **RCT-BACKED, n = 10/arm.**
- **Anderson et al. (2019)** (§1b): for *maintenance* of CPR, **monthly ≫ 3-monthly ≈ 6-monthly ≈ yearly** at 12 months. Fixed intervals; the "expanding" question was not asked. **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Boutin, Panzer & Blandin (2013),** *Human Movement Science* 32(6):1201–1213 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Testing during early practice improved transfer at 10 min and at 24 h, but did not add to the offline (between-session) gain; i.e. retrieval helps *encoding*, not consolidation per se. **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Direct expanding-vs-uniform test for a motor skill: none found** (searched surgical, music, sport, and rehabilitation literatures; the term "expanding" in that literature refers to spaced *retraining* schedules with fixed intervals). This is a genuine gap, not a search failure I can rule out entirely, so: **(unverified) that no such study exists; SPECULATIVE that the verbal null transfers.**

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- The verbal literature's most consistent moderator is **whether the first re-test would succeed**: expanding helps when initial learning is weak (Toppino 2018), interference is high (Storm 2010) and feedback is absent (Cepeda 2006's hypothesis). A dance figure that was learned to a proficiency criterion in session one is closer to Toppino's "high-level initial training" condition, where expanding conferred nothing.
- The motor comparisons above (weekly vs monthly; 1–2 vs 6–8 days; 1 vs 2 vs 7 days) all involve skills trained to criterion and retention intervals ≤ 4 months. None tests the *maintenance* regime beyond a few months, where the decay literature (§3) says the interval ceiling actually binds.
- **Sessions count, distribution pattern doesn't** (Walsh 2023, verbal; Baddeley & Longman 1978, typing) is the most transferable summary.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Do not treat "the interval must grow after every success" as evidence-based for a figure.** Treat it as one acceptable way to *reduce the number of sessions* a stable figure consumes, which is a scheduling-budget argument, not a learning argument.
- **During acquisition, a fixed 1–3 day gap is as well supported as anything** (Yang 2023; Tang 2023; Simmons 2012; Fahl 2023). Don't stretch the gap just because the last rep was good.
- **A stable figure at a fixed 7-day gap and a fixed 30-day gap tied** in the one study that tried (Mitchell 2011, n = 24) — so a coarse ladder (2 d → 7 d → 14–30 d) will not be distinguishable from an SM-2 exponent in practice, and is easier to reason about.

## 3. The acquisition-speed vs retention trade-off — and how fast motor skills decay

**Finding.** The "learning vs performance" dissociation (parent §4: Schmidt & Bjork 1992; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015) is the frame; this section adds the *forgetting* side. Motor skills decay much more slowly than facts, decay depends heavily on task type, and overlearning slows decay — so the "keep it for life" schedule can be far sparser than a flashcard schedule.

**Evidence.**
- **Arthur, Bennett, Stanush & McNelly (1998),** *Human Performance* 11(1):57–101 — **VERIFIED (abstract; full text OCR-garbled).** 189 data points from 53 articles. Skill loss ranges from **d = −0.01 immediately after training to d ≈ −1.4 after > 365 days** of non-use. **Physical, natural and speed-based tasks were less susceptible to loss than cognitive, artificial and accuracy-based tasks.** Closed-loop (fixed-sequence, discrete) tasks were expected and found to decay faster than open-loop (continuous, e.g. tracking) tasks; degree of overlearning (Driskell et al.'s operationalisation) was a moderator. **META-BACKED.** *(Per-category d values: (unverified) — the moderator table did not survive extraction; the abstract statements above are what I stand behind.)*
- **Tatel & Ackerman (2025),** *Psychological Bulletin* (accepted manuscript; DOI 10.1037/bul0000481) — **VERIFIED (author PDF).** The successor meta-analysis: **1,344 effect sizes from 457 reports**, retention interval modelled *continuously*. Decay strengthens by **0.08 SD per month** for accuracy measures and **0.06 SD per month** for speed/mixed measures; **half of initial acquisition gains lost at ≈ 6.5 months (accuracy), 13 months (speed), 11 months (mixed)**; all accuracy gains gone at ≈ 16 months. Moderators: laboratory/artificial tasks decay faster than every real-world task type; **higher component complexity → better retention** (the opposite of the intuition that complex things are forgotten first); intermittent performance opportunities and task instructions also moderate. They also flag that most "retention" tests contain relearning and that speed–accuracy trade-offs distort decay estimates. **META-BACKED** — the single best current estimate of the motor forgetting curve.
- **Driskell, Willis & Copper (1992),** *Journal of Applied Psychology* 77(5):615–622 — **VERIFIED (title/venue; findings via Semantic Scholar record).** 15 studies; overlearning improves retention with a moderate overall effect, moderated by degree of overlearning, task type and retention interval; **smaller effect for physical than cognitive tasks**, and the benefit **fades as the retention interval lengthens**. **META-BACKED.**
- **Continuous vs discrete.** **Adams (1987),** *Psychological Bulletin* 101(1):41–74 — **VERIFIED (citation; full text not read).** The historical review that consolidated the 1950s–60s finding that continuous tracking-type skills are retained for months to years while discrete button-press-type sequences decay. **Fleishman & Parker (1962),** *Journal of Experimental Psychology* 64(3):215–226 — **VERIFIED (citation).** Complex continuous tracking retested at 1–24 months; retention "extremely great," first decrement at 14 months; retention predicted by initial proficiency, not training method. **PRIMARY.** For sequence skills the picture is mixed: **Willingham & Dumas (1997),** *Psychological Research* 60:72–79 — **VERIFIED (title/venue).** Implicit SRT sequence knowledge **not** retained after one year; but **Romano, Howard & Howard (2010),** *Memory* 18(4):427–441 — **VERIFIED (abstract)** — found sequence-specific SRT retention at one year in young and older adults. **CONTESTED** for lab sequences; the applied picture (Baddeley & Longman's ~30 % typing-speed loss over 1–9 months; Tatel & Ackerman's half-life of 6–13 months) is the safer anchor. *Pointer: Schmidt & Lee, Motor Control and Learning (Human Kinetics), retention & transfer chapter — (unverified edition/pages).*
- **Does massed practice get you good *faster*?** Not per rep, and not per session, in the motor data. Within-session: distributed > massed at end of acquisition (Lee & Genovese 1988, ES 0.96; Donovan acquisition d = 0.45). Between-session: distributed ≥ massed during acquisition in Shea 2000, Dail & Christina 2004, Moulton 2006 (immediate post-test equal), Krigolson 2021 (early-session advantage). The only sense in which massing is "faster" is that it packs the reps into fewer calendar days. **RCT/META-BACKED.**

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- Decay estimates are dominated by tasks that are **not** practised at all during the interval. A dancer who dances *other* figures every week is in Tatel & Ackerman's "intermittent use" regime, and figures that share components with what she is dancing are being partially rehearsed (see Shea & Kohl 1991, §6 caveats). Expect *slower* decay than the meta-analytic curve for any figure embedded in a routine she dances.
- The complexity finding (more complex → better retained) is about *component* complexity of a coherent skill; it is not a licence to think a badly learned complex figure is safe.
- "Physical tasks decay less" and "physical tasks gain less from overlearning" are both in the data — the second is why endless drilling of an already-clean figure has diminishing returns for retention.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Calendar time is cheap for motor skills.** A figure that is genuinely clean can go weeks without practice before measurable loss; the app should not nag at flashcard cadences.
- **Discrete, choreography-like items (a new amalgamation, a sequence of steps) sit on the fast-decay side; embodied continuous skills (sway, rise-and-fall, frame) on the slow side.** Per-card decay assumptions should differ by *kind* of card, not just by difficulty.
- **Overlearn to the point of "clean twice in a row across two sessions," then stop drilling and start spacing** — extra drilling of a physical skill buys less retention than the same reps spread later (Driskell 1992 + §1).

## 4. Consolidation and the minimum useful gap

**Finding.** There is a floor under the inter-session gap. Motor memories are labile for hours after practice, and repeating the same skill within that window mostly restores performance lost to fatigue rather than adding learning. A night's sleep between sessions is the best-supported "minimum gap"; a same-day second session is not worthless but is worth less than the same session tomorrow.

**Evidence.**
- **Brashers-Krug, Shadmehr & Bizzi (1996),** *Nature* 382:252–255 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Force-field reaching: learning an opposing field **immediately** after the first disrupted consolidation of the first; **no disruption if 4 h elapsed**, with consolidation "occurring gradually over this period." **PRIMARY.** The origin of the "4–6 h window" claim.
- **Caithness, Osu, Bays, Chase, Klassen, Kawato, Wolpert & Flanagan (2004),** *Journal of Neuroscience* 24(40):8662–8671 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Three labs, ABA paradigms in visuomotor rotation and force fields: **B fully interfered with A even 24 h or 1 week later**, including when anterograde interference was controlled with washout trials. "Our results fail to support the idea that motor memories become consolidated into a protected state." **PRIMARY — CONTESTED.** **Krakauer & Shadmehr (2006),** *Trends in Neurosciences* 29(1):58–64 — **VERIFIED (citation).** The review that lays out the dispute and the "anterograde interference masks consolidation" rebuttal. Net: the *existence* of an interference-sensitive window is supported; its *duration* and whether it ever closes for opposing adaptations is unresolved.
- **Rickard, Cai, Rieth, Jones & Ard (2008),** *JEP:LMC* 34(4):834–842 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Identified four artefacts behind "sleep enhances motor sequences": averaging masks learning, averaging masks **reactive inhibition**, time-of-day confounds, and **fatigue build-up over massed training**; controlling them eliminated the sleep enhancement. "Sleep may play a role in protection from forgetting … it does not result in performance enhancement." **PRIMARY.** **Rieth et al. (2010),** *Behavioural Brain Research* 214:470–474 — spacing trials within a session removed both the massed-practice fatigue and the "offline gain." **PRIMARY.** **Pan & Rickard (2015),** *Psychological Bulletin* 141(4):812–834 — **VERIFIED (abstract)**; 34 articles / 1,296 subjects; after confound control, no compelling evidence of sleep-specific enhancement. **META-BACKED / CONTESTED** (see parent §3 for the pro-enhancement side: Walker et al. 2002/2003; the stabilisation reading of Nettersheim et al. 2015).
  - **Why this matters for scheduling:** the same data that debunk "sleep adds 20 %" also show that **a short rest within a session recovers performance without adding learning**. A dancer who looks better after a ten-minute break has shed reactive inhibition, not learned more. Do not let the app read a within-session "recovery" as evidence the card is stronger.
- **Simmons (2012)** (§2b): three sessions at **5 min / 6 h / 24 h** — accuracy gains only with 24 h (sleep) gaps; speed gains with 6 h or 24 h. **RCT-BACKED.** **Kwon et al. (2015)** (§1b): 12 h with sleep beat 10 min. **Spruit et al. (2017)** (§1b): a 35-min nap in a break partly rescued a massed schedule; consecutive days beat everything. **Tang et al. (2023)** (§2b): 1–2 days ≈ 6–8 days once sleep intervenes.
- **Boutin et al. (2013)** (§2b): the between-session offline gain was **not** amplified by testing during practice — retrieval acts at encoding, sleep/consolidation acts on whatever was encoded. **RCT-BACKED.**
- Sleep specifics (stage-2 NREM, overnight gain size) — see parent §3, TL;DR #7; not repeated.

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- The interference findings are from **adaptation** tasks (opposing force fields, rotations) where A and B are literally contradictory mappings. Two dance figures are rarely opposing in that sense; the closest dance analog is learning the *mirror* or a *near-variant* (same entry, different exit) of a figure just learned. Whether that interferes within hours is **SPECULATIVE**.
- The "4–6 h" number should not be engineered around. Both the pro (Brashers-Krug) and con (Caithness) camps agree that *immediately* practising a competing mapping is bad; they disagree whether any delay fixes it.
- "Same-day is wasted" is too strong: same-day repetition produced *speed* gains in Simmons and partial benefit in Spruit's nap group. It is *less efficient*, not useless.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Never schedule the same card twice in one day as if it were two repetitions.** Count a day as one exposure for scheduling purposes, regardless of reps.
- **Minimum gap = 1 night** for anything the app calls "a repetition."
- **Avoid introducing a near-variant of a brand-new figure in the same session** (e.g. natural and reverse versions of a new figure on the same day) until the first version has survived one sleep. **SPECULATIVE**, cheap to honour.
- Treat within-session rest as a fatigue tool, not a learning tool; the companion file (`withinSessionPracticeStructure.md`) covers that.

## 5. Retrieval practice / the testing effect in motor skills

**Finding.** Performing a skill *from memory* — without the demonstration, video, or coach's prompt in front of you — is a retrieval event, and retrieval strengthens motor memory more than re-exposure does. This is the mechanism that makes spacing work (you cannot retrieve what is still in front of you), and it is why the gap should be as long as retrieval still succeeds.

**Evidence.**
- **Kromann, Jensen & Ringsted (2009),** *Medical Education* 43(1):21–27 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** 140 medical students, 4-h resuscitation course; the intervention group replaced the last 30 min of practice with a **test**. At 2 weeks: **82.8 % vs 73.3 %** on a scenario checklist, **effect size 0.93**, p < .001. **RCT-BACKED.** **Kromann, Bohnstedt, Jensen & Ringsted (2010),** *Advances in Health Sciences Education* 15(3):395–401 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Same design, 6-month retention: 75.9 vs 70.3, **effect size 0.4, p = .06**. **RCT-BACKED** (underpowered; the effect shrank with time but did not reverse).
- **Tempel, Loran & Frings (2015),** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 21(3):300–312 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** **Dance figures** (salsa / kizomba categories). Selectively *retrieving* a subset of figures improved their later recall **and induced forgetting of related, non-retrieved figures**; selective *restudy* improved the restudied figures **without** the forgetting side-effect. The authors conclude the affected representation is "in a format closely corresponding to parameters of movement execution" and stress "the importance of separating restudy and retrieval practice when teaching people new movements." **RCT-BACKED — the one directly dance-specific study in this brief.**
- **Boutin, Panzer & Blandin (2013)** (§2b): testing during early practice produced rapid within-practice gains and better effector transfer at 10 min and 24 h; it did not amplify the offline gain. **RCT-BACKED.**
- **Soares, Pedrosa, Sandars & Cecílio-Fernandes (2024),** *Medical Teacher* — **VERIFIED (abstract).** Quasi-experiment (13 vs 18 nursing students): combined spacing + testing prevented 3-month decay of BLS skills where a single session without testing did not; results for ALS were mixed. **Weak (small, non-randomised) — pointer only.**
- **Mechanism — Benjamin & Tullis (2010),** *Cognitive Psychology* 61(3):228–247 — **VERIFIED (abstract).** The **reminding model**: a later study event helps to the extent it *reminds* the learner of the earlier one, i.e. triggers a retrieval; too-short gaps give trivially easy reminding (little benefit), too-long gaps give failed reminding (no benefit). Explains superadditivity and the non-monotonic (inverted-U) spacing curve that encoding-variability theories cannot. **THEORY-DERIVED (verbal).** Its scheduling implication: **the best gap is the longest one at which the reminding/retrieval still succeeds** — which is what Storm et al. 2010 and Toppino et al. 2018 (§2a) test.
- **Ruitenburg et al. (2025),** *Applied Cognitive Psychology* (DOI 10.1002/acp.70032) — **VERIFIED (title/venue).** Mathematical procedures in schoolchildren: spacing benefit was largest when initial practice performance was **~35–75 %**; below that, re-exposure and shorter gaps were needed; above, longer gaps. **RCT-BACKED (procedural-cognitive, not motor)** — the closest thing to an empirical "retrieval-success window."

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- Kromann's "test" was a scored scenario with assessors — high-stakes retrieval. Whether an unobserved "do it from memory" rep in a studio carries the same weight is **SPECULATIVE**; the parent brief's estimation protocol (parent §2) is the mechanism that would make it count.
- Tempel et al.'s retrieval-induced forgetting is a **warning as well as a recommendation**: selectively retrieving *some* figures of a family suppresses the rest for a while. In a session that drills one variation of a figure family, the un-drilled siblings may be temporarily *harder* to recall. Whether this persists across days in dancers is unknown.
- The reminding model is a verbal-memory theory; its application to motor programs is an analogy supported by Tempel's results, not a tested claim.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **The unit of practice the app schedules should be "execute from memory, then check," not "watch, then copy."** Re-watching the reference video before the first attempt converts a retrieval event into restudy (Tempel 2015 shows restudy is the *weaker* of the two).
- **Ask "could I do it without the video?" as the card's rating question** — it is the motor analog of a recall test and directly probes retrieval success (§7).
- **When drilling one variation of a figure family, briefly retrieve the siblings too** at the end of the session, to counter retrieval-induced forgetting. **SPECULATIVE.**

## 6. Practical scheduling algorithms and what they assume

**Finding.** SM-2, Leitner and FSRS were all built for *declarative, binary-recall* items with a fast forgetting curve, item independence and a "lapse = forgotten" model. Each of those assumptions is wrong or unverified for a dance figure. That does not make the algorithms useless — their *structure* (per-item difficulty, interval grows with demonstrated strength, lapse shortens the interval) is sound engineering — but their *constants and update rules* have no motor validation.

### 6a. The three algorithms, briefly

- **Leitner (1972),** *So lernt man lernen* — **(unverified original; description via secondary sources).** Boxes of increasing review interval; a correct recall promotes the card one box, a failure demotes it to box 1. Original partitions 1/2/5/8/14 cm, review when a partition fills. Assumptions: binary recall, one global ladder, failure resets to the start. **THEORY-DERIVED (practitioner).**
- **SM-2 (Wozniak 1990; SuperMemo spec)** — **VERIFIED (supermemo.com "SuperMemo 2: Algorithm").** Per-item **easiness factor EF** (start 2.5; floor 1.3). Intervals **I(1) = 1 day, I(2) = 6 days, I(n) = I(n−1) × EF**. After each review rated **q ∈ 0–5**, **EF′ = EF + (0.1 − (5−q)(0.08 + (5−q)·0.02))** (unchanged at q = 4). **q < 3 restarts the item at I(1)** without changing EF. Assumptions: exponential interval growth, a 0–5 self-rating that is really "was recall correct and how hard," lapse = full reset. **Wozniak & Gorzelańczyk (1994),** *Acta Neurobiologiae Experimentalis* 54:59–62 — **VERIFIED (abstract)** — the later two-component (stability / retrievability) model that underlies SuperMemo's successors; formula fitted for 95 % retention of paired associates. **PRIMARY (verbal).**
- **FSRS (open-spaced-repetition; algorithm wiki) and Ye, Su & Cao (2022),** *Proc. 28th ACM SIGKDD*, pp. 4381–4390 — **VERIFIED (ACM abstract; wiki).** Memory state = **Difficulty D ∈ [1,10]**, **Stability S** (the interval at which retrievability = 90 %), **Retrievability R(t,S)**. Forgetting curve: FSRS-4 R = (1 + t/9S)⁻¹; FSRS-4.5+ a power law R = (1 + F·t/S)^(−0.5). After a successful review S grows by a factor that is **larger when D is low, larger when R at review was low (the spacing effect), and with diminishing returns at high S**; after a lapse S is re-estimated from D, previous S and R at failure (much lower than the success path). Ratings **again / hard / good / easy**; the user picks a **desired retention** (default 0.9) and the next interval is the t at which R falls to it. The KDD paper fits a Markov memory model to **220 million** MaiMemo review logs and uses a stochastic-shortest-path solver to minimise total review cost, reporting a 12.6 % improvement over prior schedulers. **PRIMARY (verbal / language-learning data).**

### 6b. Assumption by assumption, against the motor evidence

| Assumption | Holds for facts | For a dance figure |
|---|---|---|
| **Binary recall** (remembered / forgot) | Yes by construction | **No.** Motor performance is graded on speed, accuracy, smoothness, and partner/musical fit; it *degrades* rather than vanishes (Arthur 1998; Tatel & Ackerman 2025). A figure is never "again" in the flashcard sense unless the sequence itself is lost. |
| **Forgetting-curve time constant** (days) | R drops to 0.9 in ~1–10 days for new items | **Wrong scale.** Motor decay ≈ 0.06–0.08 SD/month; half of gains lost at 6–13 months; continuous skills barely decay (§3). Fitting FSRS's curve would need motor data that do not exist. |
| **Item independence** | Approximately (interference aside) | **No.** Figures share components; practising related tasks in the gap *improved* retention of the target (Shea & Kohl 1991, *RQES* 62:187–195 — **VERIFIED (abstract)**), and selective retrieval of some figures *suppressed* their siblings (Tempel 2015). Cards in the same family are coupled both ways. |
| **Performance at review = memory strength** | Roughly | **Unreliable.** Within-session performance is contaminated by warm-up, fatigue/reactive inhibition (Rickard 2008) and by the learning–performance dissociation (parent §4). A rating taken at the end of a long session is systematically pessimistic; one taken after a break is optimistic. |
| **Lapse ⇒ reset to day 1** | Reasonable | **Too harsh.** Motor relearning shows large savings; a "failed" retention test is typically followed by rapid re-attainment (Andersen 2016's massed group recovered 1.8 points between two retention procedures; Arthur 1998 discusses reacquisition). A lapse should *shorten* the interval, not restart the ladder. |
| **Expanding intervals are optimal** | Not really even for facts (§2a: g ≈ 0.03 vs uniform) | **Untested for motor.** Fixed 2-day beat fixed 7-day during acquisition (Yang 2023); fixed weekly tied fixed monthly for a stable skill (Mitchell 2011). |
| **Desired retention 90 %** has meaning | Yes (probability of recall) | **No motor equivalent** exists. The nearest usable target is "probability the dancer can perform it cleanly without the video," which is exactly the self-rating in §7. |

### 6c. Published attempts to use spaced-repetition logic for motor / procedural skills

- **Resuscitation training** is the only domain with RCT-level evidence: fixed-interval "low-dose, high-frequency" refreshers (monthly ≫ quarterly at 12 months, Anderson et al. 2019; 15/17 studies favour spaced, Yeung et al. 2020 with very-low certainty). None uses an adaptive SM-2/FSRS-style scheduler; all use **fixed calendar intervals**. **RCT-BACKED (fixed intervals only).**
- **Surgical simulation:** spaced retraining at fixed 1-day / 2-day / 1-week intervals (Yang 2023), weekly vs monthly (Mitchell 2011), 1–2 vs 6–8 days (Tang 2023) — again fixed intervals; no adaptive algorithm. **RCT-BACKED (fixed).**
- **Music practice apps** (Phiano, MemoRep, Piano Practice Assistant) market SM-2-style scheduling of pieces and passages; **I found no peer-reviewed evaluation** of any of them. **(unverified — practitioner tools.)**
- **No peer-reviewed study applying SM-2, Leitner or FSRS to a motor skill** turned up in surgical, sport, rehabilitation, music or dance searches. **(unverified absence; treat any such claim in app marketing as unsupported.)**

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Borrow the structure, not the constants.** Per-card difficulty, interval grows with demonstrated strength, lapse shortens the interval — keep. SM-2's 1 → 6 → ×EF ladder, FSRS's 90 % target and its fitted power-law decay — do not import; they encode the wrong time-constant and the wrong success criterion.
- **Replace "recall probability" with "can perform without reference," rated on a graded scale**, and let the rating drive the *next fixed gap from a small ladder* rather than a continuous exponent (§7).

## 7. Design implications for the app

The app today: kanban columns with a `due_offset_days`; moving a card into a column schedules it that many days out; cards carry `last_practiced_on` and `due_date`; sessions pick due cards. What follows separates **what the evidence supports** (labelled by the strongest applicable label) from **reasonable engineering defaults** (labelled SPECULATIVE). Within-session rules — how many reps before switching, interleaving, session length — are in the companion `withinSessionPracticeStructure.md` and are not repeated here.

### 7a. A phase model

**Phase 1 — Acquiring** (card is new, or the dancer cannot yet perform it from memory).
- **Gap: fixed 1–3 days, never twice in a day, always with sleep between.** Supported: consecutive-day spacing is the best-tested regime (Fahl 2023; Verdaasdonk 2007; Spruit 2017), 2-day ≥ 1-day > 1-week for acquisition (Yang 2023), same-day repeats add little (Simmons 2012; Rickard 2008). **RCT-BACKED (surgical/lab tasks); SPECULATIVE in dance.**
- **Do not expand the gap on success here.** Expanding conferred nothing after solid initial training (Toppino 2018, verbal) and there is no motor evidence for it. **THEORY-DERIVED.**
- **Exit criterion: performed cleanly from memory at the start of two separate sessions** (i.e. across a sleep, before any warm-up rep on that card). This is the app-level version of "retention test, not drill" (parent §4) and of Andersen 2016's "needs a warm-up rep" signal. **SPECULATIVE default.**

**Phase 2 — Consolidating** (performable from memory; not yet automatic or not yet clean in context).
- **Gap: a coarse ladder, e.g. 3 → 7 → 14 days.** Weekly and monthly tied for a stable surgical skill (Mitchell 2011); the verbal ridgeline (Cepeda 2008) would allow ~7–14 days for a 6–8-week horizon. Whether the steps grow geometrically or arithmetically is undetectable in the data; a ladder with three rungs is honest about that. **RCT-BACKED direction; SPECULATIVE rungs.**
- **A "hard but clean" rating holds the rung; "clean and easy" advances one rung; "needed the video / broke down" drops one rung** (not to the bottom — §6b lapse row). **SPECULATIVE.**

**Phase 3 — Maintaining** (automatic; part of routines).
- **Gap: fixed long interval with a cap — ~14–30 days — not an ever-expanding one.** Motor decay is slow (0.06–0.08 SD/month) but monthly refreshers beat quarterly by a wide margin for CPR (Anderson 2019), and the CPR result is the only long-horizon dose-response we have. Uncapped SM-2 intervals (months to years) are appropriate for facts with savings-free forgetting; for a discrete figure whose half-life is ~6–13 months, a cap around 30 days keeps every card inside its retrieval-success window with margin. **RCT-BACKED (CPR) / META-BACKED (decay); SPECULATIVE cap.**
- **Continuous skill cards** (frame, sway, footwork quality) can sit at the long end of that cap or beyond; **discrete sequence cards** (a named figure, an amalgamation) at the short end. Kind-of-card should set the cap, not only difficulty (§3). **META-BACKED distinction; SPECULATIVE mapping.**
- **Cards embedded in a routine the dancer performs regularly are being rehearsed implicitly**; the app could treat "danced in a run-through" as a partial exposure that pushes `due_date` out but does not reset the ladder. **SPECULATIVE.**

**Lapses.** A card that cannot be performed from memory at the start of a session goes **back one phase** (Maintaining → Consolidating at the 3-day rung; Consolidating → Acquiring), not to "new." Motor relearning is fast (§6b); a full reset over-schedules. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

### 7b. The rating signal — what replaces binary recall

The evidence supports one question above all others: **"Could I do it without the video / without being shown?"** — because retrieval, not re-exposure, is what strengthens motor memory (§5), and because it is the motor analog of a recall probability (§6b). A workable graded scale, in the spirit of SM-2's q but re-worded for motor skills:

1. **Couldn't start it** without the reference → lapse.
2. **Got through it with the reference** (watched first, then did it) → restudy; counts as an exposure, not a retrieval; hold or drop a rung.
3. **Did it from memory, but broke down / had to think** → retrieval succeeded with difficulty → hold the rung (this is the *desirable* difficulty; Storm 2010 / Benjamin & Tullis 2010 say this is where spacing pays).
4. **Did it from memory, clean** → advance a rung.
5. **Clean, automatic, could do it while talking / at tempo / with a partner variation** → advance, and eligible for the Maintaining cap.

Two design rules that the evidence directly motivates:
- **Take the rating from the *first* attempt of the session on that card, before any warm-up rep.** Later attempts are contaminated by within-session recovery (Rickard 2008) and by performance-not-learning (parent §4). **PRIMARY-derived.**
- **Log "watched the reference before attempting" as its own flag.** It separates restudy from retrieval (Tempel 2015) and tells the scheduler the rating is a restudy rating. **RCT-BACKED distinction.**
- Optional secondary signals: rep count to first clean execution (a proxy for warm-up cost — Andersen 2016), and the parent brief's error-estimation hit-rate (parent §2) if the app ever captures a prediction. **SPECULATIVE.**

### 7c. Why per-card difficulty matters more than the global formula

Every algorithm in §6 that survived contact with real data did so because of **per-item difficulty**, not because of its interval formula: SM-2's EF, FSRS's D (and FSRS's reported gains over SM-2 come largely from better *per-item* state estimation, not from a different schedule shape). The motor literature points the same way: retention varies more by *task kind* (continuous vs discrete, natural vs artificial, component complexity — Arthur 1998; Tatel & Ackerman 2025) and by *individual initial proficiency* (Fleishman & Parker 1962; Ruitenburg 2025) than by any schedule manipulation tested. And the schedule-shape manipulations themselves mostly tie (§2). So: **spend design effort on getting each card's phase, kind, and last-first-attempt rating right; spend little on the exact interval arithmetic.** **META-BACKED reasoning; the design conclusion is SPECULATIVE.**

### 7d. What the current `due_offset_days`-per-column model can and cannot express

**Can express:**
- A fixed-interval ladder. Columns = rungs (e.g. "Daily" 1–2 d, "Every few days" 3 d, "Weekly" 7 d, "Fortnightly" 14 d, "Monthly" 30 d). Because the motor evidence favours fixed rungs over continuous exponents (§2, §7a), **this is closer to the evidence than an SM-2 port would be.** Moving a card right = advancing a rung; moving it left = a lapse handled gracefully (one rung, not to zero).
- The Maintaining cap — it is simply the rightmost column.
- Sessions picking due cards = a retrieval-first session, provided the session UI asks for a from-memory attempt before showing the reference.

**Cannot express (yet):**
- **The one-night minimum.** A column with `due_offset_days = 0` or a second practice on the same day would count as a repetition; the evidence says it should not (§4). Needs a rule: `due_date ≥ last_practiced_on + 1`, and multiple practices on one date collapse to one exposure.
- **Kind-of-card.** The same column implies the same gap for a continuous-quality card and a discrete-sequence card; §3 says their decay differs. Either separate ladders per kind, or a per-card multiplier on the column offset.
- **Rating-driven movement.** Today the *user* moves cards between columns; the evidence-backed signal (§7b) would let the app *propose* the move (advance / hold / drop) from the first-attempt rating, with the human still deciding. This keeps per-card difficulty implicit in *which column the card lives in*, which is fine — but it means the app should never auto-expand beyond the next column.
- **Family coupling.** Related figures practised in the gap partially maintain each other (Shea & Kohl 1991) and selective retrieval suppresses siblings (Tempel 2015). A "family" tag that (a) nudges the scheduler to co-schedule siblings within a week and (b) treats a run-through of the routine as a partial exposure for its member figures would express this. **SPECULATIVE.**
- **Phase.** A card's phase is currently implied by its column. That works if columns are phases; it breaks if the same offset is used for "new, do daily" and "maintaining, do daily because competition is next week." A small explicit phase field would let the scheduler apply the right lapse rule.

**What not to build:** an FSRS-style fitted forgetting curve with a desired-retention slider. There is no motor dataset to fit it to, the time-constant is wrong by an order of magnitude, and the verbal evidence says the schedule shape it optimises (expanding) is not the lever that matters. If the app ever collects enough first-attempt ratings per card, the *first* thing worth fitting is the per-kind decay rate, not the schedule.
