# The Self-Assessment Signal

*What an app should ask a dancer after she practises a figure alone — and how much to trust the answer — so that scheduling and progression can be driven by it. Companion to `motorLearningForDance.md` (referenced below by section number); between-session spacing, within-session structure and periodization are covered in their own companion files and are not repeated here.*

---

## Scope & method

Same conventions as `motorLearningForDance.md` (Scope & method, evidence labels PRIMARY / RCT-BACKED / META-BACKED / THEORY-DERIVED / CONTESTED / SPECULATIVE / (unverified)). Every load-bearing claim was traced to the abstract or full text via Europe PMC, Crossref, PMC, or the author's PDF; where only bibliographic data could be confirmed the content claim is marked (unverified). One caveat specific to this brief: the metacognition literature is overwhelmingly **verbal** (word pairs, vocabulary). Where a finding is verbal-only I say so explicitly, because the one place it has been tested carefully in motor learning (Simon & Bjork 2001/2002) the verbal shortcut — "just delay the judgment" — did **not** rescue accuracy.

The question this brief answers: a fact-SRS gets a binary or 4-point recall grade after each retrieval and that grade drives the scheduler well. A dancer practising alone has no answer key. How trustworthy is her self-rating, which format is least biased, and what cheap objective signals can stand beside it?

---

## TL;DR — ranked by leverage for the app

1. **"How did that feel?" measured immediately after warm, blocked reps is the single most misleading signal you could collect — it tracks today's fluency, which is inversely related to retention.** In Simon & Bjork's keystroke-timing studies, learners in blocked practice predicted the *best* next-day performance and delivered the *worst*; the gap between predicted and actual error was ~11 % of target time after blocked practice vs ~4 % after random practice (medium–large effect), and a fresh judgment made a day later, right before the test, was *still* wrong. *(Simon & Bjork 2001, 2002 — PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.)* Feedback framing alone moves the rating with zero change in learning. *(Carter, Smith & Ste-Marie 2016 — PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.)*

2. **Make the rating a *cold retrieval attempt*, not a feeling.** The signal that generalises from the verbal SRS world is not the grade but the *test*: retrieval attempts predict retention (Nelson & Dunlosky 1991, delayed-JOL, verbal) and improve it (Kromann et al. 2009, resuscitation skills, *d* = 0.93). Ask the dancer to *predict* whether the first attempt of the session will be clean, do it cold (no demo, no warm-up reps of that figure), then record what happened. Prediction-then-outcome is also the mechanism that made 100 % feedback the *best* condition in Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001 (existing §2). **RCT-BACKED for the mechanism; the specific app protocol is SPECULATIVE.**

3. **Expect systematic overestimation and expect it to be worst in the least skilled.** Physician self-assessment vs observed competence: 13 of 20 comparisons showed little, no, or an *inverse* relationship, worst among the least skilled and most confident. *(Davis et al. 2006 — META-BACKED.)* Surgical technical skills: accuracy improves with experience and with video playback; the typical error is overestimation; stress reduces accuracy. *(Nayar et al. 2020 — META-BACKED.)* Educational self-assessment overall: *r* ≈ 0.44 with expert scores, overestimation *g* ≈ 0.21. *(León, Panadero & García-Martínez 2023 — META-BACKED.)* Musicians: better players are *under*confident, weaker players overrate. *(Hewitt 2015 — PRIMARY.)*

4. **Rate specific, checkable elements, not global quality.** Instrumentalists were most accurate self-rating melody and least accurate on technique/articulation. *(Hewitt 2005 — PRIMARY.)* For dance the analogue is: timing/footwork/count (checkable) vs "quality of movement" (not). **SPECULATIVE by analogy.**

5. **Objective proxies exist and are cheap: highest clean tempo, attempts to first clean rep, and whether the demo had to be re-watched.** Speed at criterion accuracy was the *only* variable that separated blocked from random practice at retention in beginning clarinettists *(Stambaugh 2011 — PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED)* and "accurate at the prescribed tempo" is the standard retention criterion in music-practice research *(Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009 — PRIMARY)*. Re-watching the demo is a retrieval failure by definition, and a demo shown *before* each rep inflates the JOL while harming retention *(Simon & Bjork 2002)*.

6. **Self-efficacy correlates with performance but has not been shown to add predictive information beyond the last performance.** It explained variance in post-test performance together with end-of-practice score *(Stevens et al. 2012 — PRIMARY)*, but the motivational pillar of OPTIMAL theory — where enhanced expectancies were supposed to *cause* learning — shrinks to "small, uncertain, potentially null" after bias correction *(McKay et al. 2022 reanalysis; Parma et al. 2023 — CONTESTED)*. Log it if you like; do not let it drive scheduling.

7. **Asking for a rating is not, by itself, harmful — but numeric grades and social comparison change the goal orientation.** Making JOLs does not on average change verbal memory (*g* = 0.05, n.s.) *(Double, Birney & Walker 2018 — META-BACKED, verbal)*; self-recording improved children's motor performance *(Kolovelonis et al. 2011 — RCT-BACKED)*. But grades produce ego-involvement and comments produce task-involvement *(Butler 1987 — RCT-BACKED, classroom)*, and pressured evaluation is on the list of things that undermine intrinsic motivation *(Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 — META-BACKED)*. Rate outcomes against the dancer's own criterion; never display a score league.

8. **Judges themselves are a noisy answer key.** Eighteen DanceSport judges rating 12 couples: mean inter-judge *r* = 0.48, Kendall's *W* = 0.58, SEM often larger than the differences between couples. *(Premelč et al. 2019 — PRIMARY.)* A coach/partner rating is better than self-rating but do not treat it as ground truth either.

## 1. How accurate is motor self-assessment?

**Finding.** Learners judge their motor learning from *current ease of execution* — and current ease is produced by exactly the practice conditions (blocked drilling, a model before every rep, feedback on good trials) that harm retention. Hence motor JOLs are not merely noisy: at the level of practice conditions they point the wrong way. Calibration improves with expertise, with video playback, and with specific rather than global criteria; it worsens under stress and among the least skilled.

**Evidence.**
- **Simon & Bjork (2001),** *J. Experimental Psychology: LMC* 27(4):907–912, "Metacognition in motor learning" — **VERIFIED** (abstract). Three keystroke patterns on a numeric keypad, each with its own sequence and total movement time; blocked vs random practice; participants periodically predicted their 24-h test performance. Blocked practice enhanced acquisition and harmed retention, yet **participants predicted better performance given blocked practice**. Authors' conclusion: humans "at their peril, interpret current ease of access to a perceptual-motor skill as a valid index of learning." **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** *(The author PDF is a scan without a text layer; the 2001 effect sizes are therefore (unverified) — the size below comes from the 2002 replication.)*
- **Simon & Bjork (2002),** *J. Experimental Psychology: Applied* 8(4):222–232 — **VERIFIED** (full text). *N* = 96 undergraduates; same task; 2 × 2 of practice schedule (blocked/random) × model (matching/mismatching timing model shown before each trial). JOLs were elicited as "if practice stopped right now and you were tested tomorrow, how close could you get to the goal time (ms)?" after every five trials and again immediately before the retention test. Results: JOLs tracked *acquisition* performance, not retention. The **predicted-minus-actual gap** (actual error larger than predicted) was **11.1 % of target time (SD 10.6) after blocked practice vs 3.7 % (SD 8.4) after random**, and **10.9 % with matching models vs 3.9 % with mismatching**; both main effects medium–large (Cohen's *f* = 0.37 and 0.35). **Crucially, the pre-retention JOL made a full day after practice was still miscalibrated** — delay alone did not restore insight. Matching models (a demo before each rep) produced lower predicted errors (6.45 % vs 9.16 %) *and* worse learning. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **Carter, Smith & Ste-Marie (2016),** *Human Movement Science* 45:63–70 — **VERIFIED.** Feedback on the three *best* vs three *worst* trials of each six-trial block; JOLs after each block and before a 24-h retention test. **No performance difference in practice or retention**, but JOLs were **significantly higher** after good-trial feedback — even when participants were told which manipulation they were in. The rating is moved by framing with no learning behind it. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **Hewitson, Al-Fawakhiri, Forrence & McDougle (2023),** *J. Neurophysiology* 130(2):264–277 — **VERIFIED.** Four visuomotor learning experiments; confidence judgments were best explained by a **recency-weighted average of recently observed errors**, re-weighted by a subjective cost function, with a shallower history when the environment was volatile. Confidence influenced behaviour only under explicit (strategic) learning. **PRIMARY.** Translation: a post-rep rating is mostly "how did the last few reps go", which is precisely the fluency cue Simon & Bjork warn about.
- **Mole, Jersakova, Kountouriotis, Moulin & Wilkie (2018),** *QJEP* 71(10):2223–2234 — **VERIFIED.** Steering in a virtual environment; participants estimated the proportion of the trial spent in the target zone. Evaluations "broadly shifted with task demands" but showed **limited calibration**; regression showed they blended direct monitoring with **task heuristics** (e.g. speed) that were often weighted inappropriately; extra auditory feedback improved evaluations by reducing that heuristic weighting. **PRIMARY.**
- **Koriat's cue-utilisation framework** — Koriat (1997), *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 126:349–370 — **VERIFIED** (title/journal; pages (unverified)). JOLs are *inferences from cues* (ease of processing, familiarity), not read-outs of memory strength; they are valid only insofar as the cue is diagnostic. Koriat & Bjork (2005), *JEP: LMC* 31(2):187–194 — **VERIFIED** citation — "illusions of competence": when the answer is in view at study, the item feels learned (foresight bias). **THEORY-DERIVED; verbal.** Simon & Bjork is the motor demonstration that the "ease" cue is *non*-diagnostic under blocked practice.
- **Music — Hewitt (2005),** *J. Research in Music Education* 53(2):148–161 — **VERIFIED.** Middle school (*N* = 92) and high school (*N* = 51) instrumentalists self-rated rehearsals while experts rated a final performance. **High school students were more accurate than middle school for all sub-areas except melody and rhythm**; both groups were **most accurate on melody and least accurate on technique/articulation**. **PRIMARY.** Hewitt (2002), *JRME* 50(3):215–226 — **VERIFIED**: junior-high students showed **no correlation** between self- and expert ratings at pretest and in most post-test sub-areas, and self-evaluation practice per se did not improve accuracy. **PRIMARY.** Hewitt (2015), *JRME* 63(3):298–313 — **VERIFIED**: *N* = 340 band students rated self-efficacy before and self-evaluated after an excerpt; **strong negative relationship between calibration bias and performance** — as ability rose, students became *under*confident; middle-school males over-rated most. **PRIMARY.**
- **Larrouy-Maestri, Wang, Vairo Nunes & Poeppel (2023),** *J. Voice* 37(4):633.e7–e14 — **VERIFIED.** Eighteen highly trained sopranos judged peers' and their own recorded pitch accuracy in pairwise comparisons; participants were "surprisingly inaccurate" about themselves and **most overestimated their own accuracy**; self-evaluation ability was related to singing proficiency. **PRIMARY.** Experts are not exempt.
- **Dance.** I found **no** study of dancer self-rating vs judge rating agreement in DanceSport or ballet **(unverified — likely gap)**. What exists is the answer-key reliability: **Premelč, Vučković, James & Leskošek (2019),** *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:1001 — **VERIFIED.** 18 judges, 12 top adult couples, 10-point scale per WDSF criterion: mean inter-judge correlation **0.48**, Kendall's *W* **0.58** overall (0.45–0.56 by component), ICC(2,3) absolute agreement 0.62, consistency 0.80; SEM often larger than between-couple differences. **PRIMARY.**
- **Procedural skills — Davis, Mazmanian, Fordis, Van Harrison, Thorpe & Perrier (2006),** *JAMA* 296(9):1094–1102 — **VERIFIED.** 17 studies, 20 comparisons of physician self-assessment vs observed competence: **13 showed little, no, or an inverse relationship**, 7 positive; worst accuracy among the **least skilled and most confident**. **META-BACKED.** **Ward, MacRae et al. (2003),** *American J. Surgery* 185(6):521–524 — **VERIFIED.** 26 senior residents, laparoscopic Nissen in a pig, expert ICC 0.66; self–expert correlation **r = 0.50 immediately, rising to 0.63 after watching their own video**; watching benchmark performances changed nothing. **PRIMARY.** **Nayar et al. (2020),** *J. Surgical Education* 77(2):348–361 — **VERIFIED.** Systematic review, 40 studies, 1,753 participants: accuracy **better with experience/age and with retrospective video playback**; the characteristic error is **overestimation**; **stressful environments reduce accuracy**. **META-BACKED.**
- **Kruger & Dunning (1999),** *J. Personality & Social Psychology* 77(6):1121–1134 — **VERIFIED.** Bottom-quartile performers overestimated their percentile by ~46 points (humour, grammar, logic — cognitive tasks, not motor). **PRIMARY**; the motor analogue is the Davis/Nayar/Hewitt-2015 pattern above.
- **Children in PE — Kolovelonis & Dimitriou (2018),** *Europe's J. Psychology* 14(3):665–679 — **VERIFIED.** 147 fifth/sixth-graders, soccer passing: self-estimates **15 % above actual** (peer estimates 48 % above), while most believed they were *worse* than average. **PRIMARY.** Kolovelonis, Goudas, Dermitzaki & Kitsantas (2012/2013), *European J. Psychology of Education* 28(3):685–701 — **VERIFIED** citation: 120 pupils, dribbling; students overestimated **except those given process goals, who underestimated**. **PRIMARY.** "Judgment of Learning in a Motor Task", *J. Teaching in Physical Education* 45(1):236– (2025) — **(unverified details; page listing only)**: 54 pupils predicting a volleyball-serve test score; the majority overestimated; whether immediate or delayed judgments were more accurate differed by grade. **PRIMARY (unverified).**
- **Educational meta-analysis — León, Panadero & García-Martínez (2023),** *Educational Psychology Review* 35:article 4 — **VERIFIED.** 160 articles, 29,352 participants; self-score vs expert score **z = 0.472 (r ≈ 0.44)**; overestimation **g = 0.206**; accuracy improved with **feedback, self-assessment experience, content knowledge**, and younger students overestimated *less*; high heterogeneity and poor reporting. **META-BACKED (not motor-specific).**

**Caveats.**
- The Simon & Bjork task is a keypad timing task; nobody has run the blocked-vs-random JOL design on whole-body choreography. The *mechanism* (ease-of-access cue) is general; the *magnitude* for dance is **(unverified)**.
- Music and surgery are the closest proxies for "codified movement judged by experts". Both show the same shape: overestimation, worse in novices, better after video.
- The dance answer key is itself unreliable (Premelč). Any "accuracy" number for dancer self-rating would have to be read against judge SEM.

**Coaching translation** (app-facing).
- Treat a post-practice rating as a measurement of **today's fluency**, not of learning. It is informative about the *opposite* of what the app wants unless the conditions that produced it are also recorded (was it warm? blocked? just after the demo?).
- Expect a novice's ratings to be inflated and a strong dancer's to be deflated; a single global rating cannot be compared across users without a per-user calibration term (§6).
- Ask about the *checkable* layer (count, footwork, timing, entry/exit) where Hewitt-style accuracy is best; do not ask for a global quality score.

## 2. Which rating format is least biased?

**Finding.** The literature does not contain a motor study comparing binary vs 4-point vs percentage self-ratings. What it does contain is stronger: evidence about *what the rating should be about* and *when it should be made*. The rating should be (a) a **retrospective judgment of a specific outcome after a retrieval attempt**, not a prospective feeling of learning; (b) made **cold** (at the start of practice on that figure, before any demo or warm-up rep), because a *delay* alone does not fix motor JOLs but a *retrieval attempt* is the diagnostic cue; (c) preceded by a **committed prediction**, which turns the rating into a learning tool (existing §2, Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001) and yields a per-user calibration score for free.

**Evidence.**
- **Prospective vs retrospective.** Nelson & Dunlosky (1991), *Psychological Science* 2(4):267–270 — **VERIFIED.** Delayed JOLs (cue only, after a delay) were nearly perfectly accurate at predicting later recall; immediate JOLs were not. **PRIMARY; verbal.** The accepted mechanism is that a delayed cue-only JOL forces a **retrieval attempt** from long-term memory. Putnam, Deng & DeSoto (2022), *Memory* 30(5):537–553 — **VERIFIED.** Three pre-registered experiments: a **retrospective confidence rating made just after a retrieval attempt predicted final-test performance at least as well as (mini-meta: slightly better than) a delayed JOL**; asking people to *predict the future* adds noise, the retrieval attempt supplies the signal. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED; verbal.**
- **But delay alone does not transfer to motor.** Simon & Bjork (2002) — the pre-retention JOL, made a day later, was as miscalibrated as those made during practice (§1). The verbal delayed-JOL effect works because the delayed judgment *is* a covert retrieval; in the motor case the participant judged before *attempting*. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** The 2025 JTPE volleyball study found no consistent immediate-vs-delayed advantage in children **(unverified details)**. → The rating must be attached to an actual **cold attempt**, not to the calendar.
- **Prediction-then-feedback as the rating.** Guadagnoli & Kohl (2001), existing §2: estimating error before feedback reversed the guidance effect — 100 % feedback with estimation was the best retention condition. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (simple task).** The app has no ground-truth feedback, but a *cold attempt* is its own feedback for the checkable layer (did the count hold? did the feet land? did I need the demo?). Recording the prediction and the outcome yields a per-figure, per-user **calibration score** (§6).
- **Metacognitive prompts / self-recording help performance.** Kolovelonis, Goudas & Dermitzaki (2011), *Learning and Instruction* 21(3):355–364 — **VERIFIED** citation; abstract via search: 105 fifth/sixth-graders, dart throwing, 3 goal types × self-recording; **self-recording improved performance**, goal type did not differ. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (children).** Kolovelonis et al. (2012/2013): process goals flipped the bias from over- to under-estimation — i.e. asking about *process* makes the learner more conservative. **PRIMARY.**
- **Ask about specific sub-components.** Hewitt (2005): accuracy varied by sub-area (best: melody; worst: technique/articulation). **PRIMARY.** By analogy, a dance rating on "count and footwork" is more trustworthy than on "quality". **SPECULATIVE.**
- **Scale granularity.** No motor comparison found **(unverified)**. Verbal: JOL scale format (percentage, Likert, yes/no) made no difference to reactivity, and the delayed-JOL/confidence advantages hold across formats — "Judgments of learning modify memory even when made covertly or using a non-numerical scale", *Metacognition and Learning* (2026) **(unverified details)**. Practitioner SRS: Anki/FSRS use four buttons where **only "Again" is a fail**; FSRS is fitted on ~700 M reviews and its documentation singles out **misuse of "Hard" as the one rating error the model cannot absorb** — a practitioner observation, not peer-reviewed **(unverified)**. Design lesson: the scale's *anchors* matter more than its point count; make the fail/pass boundary behaviourally unambiguous.
- **"Confidence I could do it in competition" vs "how did that feel".** "How did that feel" is the fluency cue (Simon & Bjork). A competition-transfer question is prospective and, per Putnam et al., adds noise; but it reframes toward the retention goal rather than today's ease. No direct test in motor **(unverified)**. Note Nayar (2020): self-assessment accuracy drops under stress — a rating taken in a stressful context is worse, not better.
- **Social-comparative framing** — Lewthwaite & Wulf (2010), *QJEP* 63(4):738–749 — **VERIFIED.** Stabilometer; false "better than average" normative feedback improved day-3 no-feedback retention vs both "worse" and veridical-only control, with more automatic (high-frequency/low-amplitude) adjustments. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** But see §5: this line sits inside the enhanced-expectancies literature that shrinks to near-null under bias correction. **CONTESTED.**

**Caveats.**
- The whole prospective/retrospective distinction has been studied with verbal materials; the motor evidence (Simon & Bjork 2002) only tells us that a *time* delay is insufficient. That a *cold attempt* restores accuracy in motor skills is an inference from the verbal mechanism plus the testing-effect data — **THEORY-DERIVED**.
- A cold attempt at the start of practice is also a mildly stressful "test"; keep the framing task-involved (§5) so the stress does not itself degrade the rating.

**Coaching translation** (app-facing).
- One question *before* the first attempt of the session on that figure ("Will the first run be clean at ___ bpm? yes / no"), then the attempt cold, then one *outcome* question with behavioural anchors. Nothing after warm reps counts toward scheduling.
- Wording targets the checkable layer: count, feet, entry/exit, tempo held. Not "quality", not "feel".

## 3. Do subjective ratings predict retention?

**Finding.** Not on their own. Immediate motor JOLs track acquisition performance, are moved by feedback framing with no learning behind them, and are anti-diagnostic across practice conditions. Self-efficacy correlates with performance (partly because it is built from it) but has not been shown to add incremental prediction of retention, and the causal claim that raising expectancies raises learning does not survive bias correction. The signal that does predict retention is the **outcome of a retrieval attempt**.

**Evidence.**
- **JOLs vs retention.** Simon & Bjork (2001, 2002) — JOLs "more in line with acquisition than with retention"; the group that predicted best retained worst. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Carter et al. (2016) — JOLs shifted by good-vs-poor feedback with identical retention. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **Self-efficacy.** Stevens, Anderson, O'Dwyer & Williams (2012), *Consciousness and Cognition* 21(3):1122–1128 — **VERIFIED.** Stick-balancing; easy or same-difficulty practice transferred, harder practice did not; self-efficacy rose modestly with easy practice and **fell significantly with difficult practice**; self-efficacy before post-test **together with** end-of-practice performance and pre-test explained **75 %** of post-test variance. **PRIMARY.** Note the direction: difficult (retention-favouring, per existing §4) practice *lowers* the rating. Hewitt (2015): strong positive relation between self-efficacy and expert-rated performance. **PRIMARY (correlational).** Saemi, Porter, Ghotbi-Varzaneh, Zarghami & Maleki (2012), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 13(4):378–382 — **VERIFIED** citation; KR after good trials produced better delayed retention *and* higher self-efficacy in a throwing task. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **The causal expectancy claim is weak.** Bacelar, Parma, Murrah & Miller (2022/2024), *Int. Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 17(1):587–616 — **VERIFIED** citation; pooled enhanced-expectancy benefit *g* ≈ 0.54 but with reporting-bias concerns. McKay, Bacelar, Parma, Miller & Carter (2022), PsyArXiv 10.31234/osf.io/3nhtc — **VERIFIED** (preprint): robust Bayesian reanalysis of the self-controlled and enhanced-expectancies meta-analyses — "the true effects are instead small, uncertain, and potentially null"; **mean statistical power across studies 6 %**. Parma, Bacelar, Cabral, Lohse, Hodges & Miller (2023), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 66:102394 — **VERIFIED.** *N* = 80, shuffleboard, easy vs hard success criterion: manipulation raised self-efficacy, perceived competence and (with 100 trials) intrinsic motivation, **with no learning benefit**; **intrinsic motivation did not predict post-test performance at the individual level**. Parma, Miller & Bacelar (2024), existing §1. **CONTESTED**, leaning null. Contrast: Iwatsuki & Regis (2021), *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 128(2):900–911 — **VERIFIED** — *N* = 30, easy criterion → better retention and transfer with higher perceived competence; small *N*, in the biased literature.
- **Perceived vs actual fluency.** Simon & Bjork: "current ease of access" is the invalid cue. Palser, Fotopoulou & Kilner (2018), *Consciousness and Cognition* 57:33–40 — **VERIFIED.** Priming people to move *faster* than baseline raised their confidence in **incorrect** decisions ("adamantly wrong") — fluency of the effector inflates confidence. **PRIMARY.**
- **Goal orientation moderates.** Meira & Fairbrother (2018), *J. Motor Learning and Development* 6(2):209–219 — **VERIFIED.** Balance task, 24-h retention/transfer without KR; **ego-oriented learners retained and transferred better** than task-oriented, mainly when KR was present in acquisition. **PRIMARY** (single study; runs against the usual assumption).
- **What does predict retention: the test itself.** Kromann, Jensen & Ringsted (2009), *Medical Education* 43(1):21–27 — **VERIFIED.** RCT, 81 students; replacing the last 30 min of a resuscitation course with a *test* raised 2-week scores from 73.3 % to 82.8 %, **effect size 0.93**. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** (Covered in the companion files; cited here only because the retrieval attempt is the signal generator.)

**Caveats.**
- No motor study was found that regressed *next-session* performance on a *post-practice self-rating* while controlling for the last attempt's outcome — the incremental validity of the rating is **(unverified / probable gap)**. Stevens (2012) is the closest, and there the rating entered alongside performance.
- Verbal SRS ratings work because they are made *after a retrieval* and are close to binary at the fail boundary. That is the property to copy, not the number of buttons.

**Coaching translation** (app-facing).
- Feed the scheduler the *outcome of the cold attempt* (and its objective proxies, §4). Treat the subjective rating as a tie-breaker and as calibration data, not as the primary interval driver.
- Expect ratings to *drop* when the app raises difficulty (Stevens 2012) — that is the challenge point working (existing §4), not a regression; do not let a falling rating trigger easier scheduling by itself.

## 4. Objective proxies without sensors

**Finding.** Three are cheap, defensible and align with how music-practice research already scores retention: **highest tempo at which the figure was clean**, **attempts to first clean rep**, and **demo re-watch (retrieval failure)**. Partner/coach ratings are better than self-ratings but noisy; video self-rating becomes usefully accurate mainly for more experienced dancers. Pose estimation is future scope.

**Evidence.**
- **Tempo reached (speed–accuracy).** Fitts (1954), *J. Experimental Psychology* 47(6):381–391 — **VERIFIED**: movement time scales with the index of difficulty; speed and accuracy trade off. **PRIMARY.** Stambaugh (2011), *J. Research in Music Education* 58(4):368–383 — **VERIFIED.** 41 beginning clarinettists, blocked vs random; no acquisition differences in accuracy, speed or evenness, but **at retention the random group played significantly faster (*F*(1,38) = 24.95, η² reported as .92 (unverified magnitude — implausibly large; treat the direction, not the number)) while holding accuracy**, and the blocked group had *slowed* from end of practice. Tempo at criterion accuracy was the only retention-sensitive measure. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009), *JRME* 56(4):310–321 — **VERIFIED**: 17 advanced pianists practised until confident they could play a passage **accurately at a prescribed tempo** next day; retention rank was unrelated to practice time or trial counts but related to error-handling strategies and to **varying tempo of problem segments systematically**. **PRIMARY.** Allingham & Wöllner (2022), *Psychology of Music* 50(6):1925–1941 — **VERIFIED**: slow practice used by 99 % of classical and 89 % of non-classical musicians; tempo management is a self-regulation strategy, not a weakness. **PRIMARY (survey).**
- **Attempts to first clean rep.** No direct study relating trials-to-criterion during a cold test to later retention was found **(unverified)**. Rationale: in verbal memory, ease of learning is a valid cue for later recall (Koriat 2008, "easily learned, easily remembered", *Memory & Cognition* 36(2):416–428 — **VERIFIED** citation), and the delayed-JOL literature shows retrieval fluency is diagnostic *after* a delay (Nelson & Dunlosky 1991). **THEORY-DERIVED**; the dance-specific version is **SPECULATIVE**.
- **Re-watching the demo.** By construction a retrieval failure; testing-effect logic says the retrieval attempt is what builds retention (Kromann 2009; Nelson & Dunlosky 1991). And Simon & Bjork (2002) show the reverse cost: a matching model before every trial **lowered predicted error (6.45 % vs 9.16 %)** while **harming** retention. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** So "had to check the demo" is both a strong negative signal and a habit the app should make slightly inconvenient.
- **Partner / coach rating.** Premelč et al. (2019): judges' *W* = 0.58, SEM larger than between-couple gaps (§1). Ward (2003): two experts' ICC = 0.66. A single coach or partner is one noisy rater; still better calibrated than the novice self-rating (Davis 2006; Nayar 2020). **PRIMARY/META-BACKED.**
- **Video self-rating accuracy** (protocol is in existing §2; only accuracy is added here). Ward (2003): self-video raised self–expert *r* from 0.50 to 0.63; benchmarks did nothing. Nayar (2020): retrospective video playback improves accuracy. Pattni et al. (2023), *PLOS ONE* 18(7):e0288474 — **VERIFIED.** 9 studies, 240 participants: 5/9 showed significant accuracy gains; **benchmark video helped those with limited experience, self-video helped the more experienced**. Scaffidi et al. (2019), *Endoscopy International Open* 7(5):E678–E684 — **VERIFIED.** RCT, 51 novices: benchmark review improved short-term accuracy, self+benchmark improved over time, **self-video alone had no effect in novices**. Silveira & Gavin (2016), *Psychology of Music* 44(4):880–892 — **VERIFIED**: 112 middle-school instrumentalists rated tone, pitch, rhythm on a 7-point scale live, after playback, and after playback two days later; ratings **changed significantly** with playback (direction of change not stated in abstract — (unverified)). **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Novices need a *reference* (benchmark) to rate their own video; experienced dancers can rate their own video usefully.
- **Pose estimation / phone sensors — future scope.** "Using AI-based feedback in dance education — a literature review", *Research in Dance Education* (2025), DOI 10.1080/14647893.2025.2524160 — **VERIFIED** citation: 17 works using OpenPose/MediaPipe/AlphaPose-style markerless pose estimation for dance feedback, typically outputting a similarity score to a reference; study designs heterogeneous. Individual systems (e.g. "DanceFormer", *Alexandria Engineering Journal* 2025; spatiotemporal-CNN quality scoring) exist but **none was found validated against expert judge ratings of dance quality (unverified)**. Smartphone video pose estimation can measure movement *frequency/tempo* reliably in a pilot (PMC8687570, 2021 — **(unverified details)**). **SPECULATIVE for the app**; tempo/period detection is the realistic first use, not "quality".

**Caveats.**
- Tempo is only meaningful with accuracy held at criterion (Fitts); log "clean at X bpm", never bare bpm.
- Every proxy above measures the *checkable* layer. Connection, weight, lead intent — the partner-dance core — remain invisible to all of them (existing §2 caveat on video).

**Coaching translation** (app-facing).
- Log per cold attempt: target bpm, clean yes/no, attempts to first clean, demo viewed before/after, duration. A "clean at target tempo on first cold attempt" is the motor analogue of "Good" in an SRS.
- Offer a benchmark clip *next to* the dancer's own clip during review for novices; let experienced dancers review self-only.

## 5. Rating and motivation

**Finding.** Asking for a rating is not intrinsically harmful; what matters is whether the rating reads as a *grade* (ego-involving, controlling) or as *information about a task* (task-involving). Numeric scores, leaderboards and false social comparison shift the goal orientation and, via the self-determination literature, can undermine intrinsic motivation — though in motor learning the motivational-pillar effects are much smaller than the coaching literature claims, in either direction.

**Evidence.**
- **Reactivity of the act of rating.** Double, Birney & Walker (2018), *Memory* 26(6):741–750 — **VERIFIED.** Meta-analysis: making JOLs had **no overall effect on memory (*g* = 0.054, 95 % CI −0.03 to 0.14)**; positive reactivity for related word pairs (*g* ≈ 0.32) and word lists, none for unrelated pairs. **META-BACKED; verbal.** No motor reactivity study found **(unverified)**. Guadagnoli & Kohl (2001) had learners estimate error on *every* trial with 100 % KR and that was the best retention cell — per-trial self-estimation did not hurt in that design. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (simple task).**
- **Self-recording / self-evaluation in PE.** Kolovelonis et al. (2011): self-recording improved dart performance in children; goal-group pupils made more technical attributions and adaptive inferences. **RCT-BACKED.** Kolovelonis, Goudas & Gerodimos (2011), *European Physical Education Review* 17(1) — **(unverified details)**: reciprocal and self-check teaching styles improved chest-pass accuracy and technique vs control. **PRIMARY.**
- **Evaluation and intrinsic motivation.** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6):627–668 — **VERIFIED.** 128 studies: expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (*d* ≈ −0.28 to −0.40 by contingency); **positive verbal feedback enhanced it (*d* = 0.33 free-choice, 0.31 interest)**. **META-BACKED.** Ryan & Deci's SDT reviews list "pressured evaluations" alongside deadlines and imposed goals as undermining — that specific claim is from the review, not this meta **(unverified as a separate effect size)**. Butler (1987), *J. Educational Psychology* 79(4):474–482 — **VERIFIED.** 200 pupils; **individual comments** produced the highest interest, performance and task-involved attributions; **numerical grades and standardised praise** produced ego-involved attributions. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (classroom).**
- **Achievement-goal orientation in motor learning.** Meira & Fairbrother (2018): ego-oriented learners retained/transferred a balance skill *better* (§3) — a caution against assuming task orientation is always superior. **PRIMARY.** Kolovelonis et al. (2012/2013): process goals shifted calibration toward underestimation — process framing makes ratings more conservative and, by the León (2023) moderators, likely more accurate. **PRIMARY.**
- **Social-comparative feedback.** Lewthwaite & Wulf (2010) — positive normative (false) feedback improved retention (§2). Chiviacowsky & Wulf (2007), *RQES* 78(2):40–47 — **VERIFIED**: KR after the best 3 of 6 trials beat KR after the worst 3 on delayed retention. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Lewis et al. (2022), *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:1005705 — **VERIFIED**: *N* = 48, joystick sequence; response-time-only feedback was *worse* than no feedback for learning; adding positive social comparison restored perceived competence but not speed. **PRIMARY.** Whole line **CONTESTED** after McKay et al. (2022) and Parma et al. (2023, 2024).

**Caveats.**
- Nothing here tests "rate after every rep vs after every block" in a motor task **(unverified)**. Nayar (2020) implies evaluation under stress *degrades the rating*; Butler and Deci imply grade-like evaluation degrades *interest*. Both argue for sparse, task-framed rating.
- The false-positive social comparison that "worked" in Lewthwaite & Wulf is deceptive by design; an app cannot ethically ship it, and the honest effect is probably small anyway.

**Coaching translation** (app-facing).
- Rate *once per figure per session*, at the cold attempt (§2). Never after every rep of a drill.
- Present the result as information ("clean at 26 bar/min, first try") and as a comment-style next step, not as a score, star or rank. No leaderboards; no cross-user comparison.
- Use genuine positive feedback on real successes (Deci: *d* ≈ 0.3) — the *cheap* part of enhanced expectancies that survives.

## 6. Design implications

Labels: strength of the evidence *for the design choice*, with SPECULATIVE wherever the step from lab to app is mine.

**6.1 The post-practice prompt — recommendation.**

- **Timing: at the *start* of the next session on that figure, not at the end of this one.** Rate on a **cold attempt**: no demo, no warm-up reps of that figure. *(RCT-BACKED for the mechanism — Simon & Bjork 2002 shows an end-of-session or next-day *feeling* is miscalibrated; Nelson & Dunlosky 1991 / Putnam 2022 show a retrieval attempt is the diagnostic cue; SPECULATIVE as an app protocol.)* An optional end-of-session "how did it feel" may be collected for the dancer's own reflection but is **excluded from scheduling**.
- **Prospective step first (one tap):** "First run at ___ bpm — will it be clean? **Yes / No**." Then the attempt. *(RCT-BACKED for prediction-before-feedback — Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001; the app version is SPECULATIVE.)* This costs nothing and produces a per-user calibration score (6.2).
- **Retrospective outcome, 4 points, behaviourally anchored, about the checkable layer:**
  1. **Clean** — count, feet, entry and exit all held at the target tempo, first attempt.
  2. **Clean after a stumble** — needed 2–3 attempts, no demo.
  3. **Broke down** — could not complete without slowing or stopping; no demo.
  4. **Had to check the demo / could not recall.**
  Anchors 3 and 4 are *fails* for the scheduler; 1 and 2 are passes; 2 is "Hard". *(THEORY-DERIVED — copies the SRS property that matters: an unambiguous fail boundary, retrospective, tied to a retrieval; point count is not evidence-based either way (unverified).)*
- **Wording targets the checkable layer** (count, feet, entry/exit, tempo held), never "quality" or "feel". *(PRIMARY by analogy — Hewitt 2005 sub-area accuracy; SPECULATIVE for dance.)*
- **Do not ask "how confident are you for competition?"** as the scheduling input: prospective, adds noise (Putnam 2022), and stress lowers accuracy (Nayar 2020). It may be asked *rarely* as a periodization signal, labelled as such. *(THEORY-DERIVED.)*

**6.2 Correcting the signal before it reaches the scheduler.**

- **Discount any rating made warm or immediately after blocked reps** — either by refusing to schedule on it or by a fixed penalty (e.g. treat "Clean" given warm as "Clean after a stumble"). *(RCT-BACKED for the bias direction — Simon & Bjork 2001/2002: ~11 % vs ~4 % of target time; the penalty size is SPECULATIVE.)*
- **Keep a per-user, per-figure calibration score** = agreement between the yes/no prediction and the cold outcome (Brier-style). Weight the *subjective* part of the rating by calibration; weight the *objective* proxies (6.3) fully. Novices start at low weight (Davis 2006; Nayar 2020; Hewitt 2015); the weight rises as calibration improves. *(META-BACKED for the direction of expertise effects; the weighting scheme is SPECULATIVE.)*
- **Expect a downward shift after difficulty increases** (Stevens 2012) and after process-framed prompts (Kolovelonis 2012/2013); do not let a falling rating alone trigger easier scheduling. *(PRIMARY for the shift; SPECULATIVE rule.)*
- **A demo view before the attempt invalidates the "Clean" rating for scheduling** (Simon & Bjork 2002: matching model → lower predicted error, worse learning). *(RCT-BACKED.)*

**6.3 Objective proxies to log (each cold attempt).**

- Target tempo and whether clean at that tempo; **highest clean tempo** as the progression variable. *(RCT-BACKED that tempo-at-criterion is retention-sensitive — Stambaugh 2011; Duke 2009; SPECULATIVE for dance figures.)*
- **Attempts to first clean rep.** *(THEORY-DERIVED; (unverified) as a retention predictor.)*
- **Demo viewed?** (before the attempt = fail for scheduling; after = fine). *(RCT-BACKED for the cost of pre-trial models; testing-effect logic.)*
- Time since last practice of the figure, and whether the attempt was the first of the session. *(Needed to interpret all of the above; THEORY-DERIVED.)*
- Optional: **partner or coach rating** on the same 4-point anchors, flagged as external; and a **video-reviewed** flag with the dancer's post-video re-rating stored separately from the pre-video one (Ward 2003: the re-rating is the more accurate one). *(PRIMARY.)*
- Future: pose-estimation *tempo/period* detection from phone video; not quality scoring. *(SPECULATIVE; (unverified) validation.)*

**6.4 What to avoid.**

- A rating after every rep of a blocked drill. *(THEORY-DERIVED — Simon & Bjork; Butler; Deci.)*
- Numeric scores, stars, streak-style grades attached to the figure, or any cross-dancer comparison. *(RCT-BACKED for goal-orientation effects — Butler 1987; META-BACKED for evaluation/reward effects — Deci 1999.)*
- Fabricated "better than average" feedback. *(Lewthwaite & Wulf 2010 is CONTESTED and deceptive by design.)*
- Showing the demo by default before the first attempt. *(RCT-BACKED — Simon & Bjork 2002.)*
- Treating a coach's or partner's single rating as ground truth. *(PRIMARY — Premelč 2019.)*
- Global "how good was that?" questions. *(PRIMARY by analogy — Hewitt 2005.)*
