# Session Composition and Periodization for the Dance Trainer

*A primary-source research brief on how to assemble one practice session and one training week from a board of dance figures — what goes in, in what order, at what new-to-established mix, and how that shifts across a competition cycle.*

---

## Scope & method

This file is a companion to `motorLearningForDance.md` and follows its conventions: every load-bearing claim traces to the original study, the researcher's own review, or a meta-analysis, read via the journal page, PubMed/PMC/Europe PMC, or the author's own PDF. Where the existing brief already covers a mechanism, this file points to it by section number (§n = `motorLearningForDance.md`) instead of repeating it.

**Deliberately out of scope** (covered by companion files): days between practices of one skill, expanding intervals, decay curves and SM-2/FSRS (`spacedRepetitionForMotorLearning.md`); reps per skill before switching, the interleaving critique, within-session revisits, reactive inhibition, session length and fatigue *during* a session (`withinSessionPracticeStructure.md`). This file is about the **composition** of a session and a week: which cards, how many, in what order, at what mix, and how that changes as a competition approaches.

**Evidence labels used inline** (same as the main brief):

- **PRIMARY** — traces to the original study / researcher's own writing.
- **RCT-BACKED** — supported by randomised controlled experiment(s).
- **META-BACKED** — supported by a meta-analysis or systematic review.
- **THEORY-DERIVED** — a framework/model, not a single decisive experiment.
- **CONTESTED** — genuine, active scientific disagreement or partial replication.
- **SPECULATIVE** — my extrapolation to dance / to the app; treat as a hypothesis to test.
- **(unverified)** — I could not confirm it from a solid source.

**Verification status.** Title/journal/year/volume confirmed against the journal page, PubMed, Europe PMC, or an author PDF for: Fradkin et al. 2010; Branscheidt et al. 2019; Alderman 1965 and Godwin & Schmidt 1971 (via PubMed/Taylor & Francis listings); Nacson & Schmidt 1971; Brashers-Krug et al. 1996; Krakauer et al. 2005; Panzer et al. 2006; Lundbye-Jensen et al. 2011; Cantarero et al. 2013; Sanli & Lee 2014; Wulf & Shea 2002; Duke et al. 2009; Carter & Grahn 2016; Farrow & Robertson 2017 (author PDF read); Otte et al. 2019 (PMC full text read); Bosquet et al. 2007; Low et al. 2021 (repository PDF read); Stoker et al. 2017 (via Low et al.'s reference list); Shibata et al. 2017; Proteau et al. 1992; Jeffries et al. 2017 and 2020; Boeding et al. 2019; Armando et al. 2025; Impellizzeri et al. 2020; Keetch & Lee 2007; Wu & Magill 2011; Sanli et al. 2013; McKay et al. 2022. Marked **(unverified)** where I could only confirm existence, not the detail cited. Two dance-pedagogy questions — intake rate of new figures per week, and choreography learning load — turned up **no quantitative primary evidence**; that absence is stated, not papered over.

**The blunt caveat, again.** Almost nothing in this file was measured on dancers, and *nothing* was measured on the assembly of whole practice sessions from a pool of skills. The strongest evidence concerns single lab tasks; the periodization frameworks are explicitly conceptual. Read every rule in §7 as "the least-wrong default the literature supports," not as a prescription.

## TL;DR — ranked by leverage for the app

1. **Interference is about *similarity*, not *count*.** Two motor memories collide when they are learned back-to-back and compete for the same effector/direction/mapping (Brashers-Krug 1996; Krakauer 2005; Lundbye-Jensen 2011 — PRIMARY). Unrelated skills coexist in one session without measurable cost. So the session-builder's guard is *"no two confusable new figures in one session"* — not a hard cap on distinct cards. **PRIMARY on the mechanism; SPECULATIVE on the dance mapping.**

2. **Put new/precision work before anything that fatigues, and finish with routines — but say why honestly.** Muscular fatigue depresses *performance* reliably (old literature) and, in the strongest modern study, impairs *learning* for days afterwards (Branscheidt 2019 — PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED, extreme fatigue protocol). A 2025 scoping review finds the fatigue→learning link inconsistent (59% negative, 27% null). The "new-early, routine-late" ordering is **CONTESTED-to-THEORY-DERIVED**, but the *downside* of the alternative is asymmetric, so it is still the right default.

3. **Warm-up is a performance and readiness effect, not a learning effect.** Warm-up improved performance in 79% of criteria across 32 studies (Fradkin 2010 — META-BACKED) and "warm-up decrement" after any rest is real and quickly reversed by task-similar activity (Nacson & Schmidt 1971 — PRIMARY). No study links warm-up to *retention*. Rule: every session opens with 1–2 *established* cards at low stakes before any card that is being scored or learned. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

4. **There is no evidence-based "N new figures per week."** Neither the motor-learning nor the music/dance pedagogy literature yields an intake rate. What exists says: novices are cognitively saturated by a single new phrase (Carey 2019, §9b), complex-skill learning needs load *reduced* not increased (Wulf & Shea 2002 — PRIMARY review), and in music the *error rate during practice*, not the quantity, predicts retention (Duke 2009 — PRIMARY). The app's new:review ratio must be a tunable default, not a claim. **SPECULATIVE default: 1–2 cognitive-stage cards per session.**

5. **Skill periodization frameworks exist, are sensible, and are untested.** Farrow & Robertson 2017 (SPORT: Specificity, Progression, Overload, Reversibility, Tedium) and Otte et al. 2019 (PoST: coordination → skill-adaptability → performance training) both state they are conceptual. Their shared, defensible direction: variability and complexity rise through the cycle; near competition, *stability and confidence* take priority over new learning, and representativeness (real floor, real conditions, pressure) peaks. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

6. **Pressure training works and has no established dose.** Meta-analysis of 14 studies (n = 394): g = 0.67 [0.41, 0.94] on high-pressure post-tests, for novices and experts, open and closed tasks; doses ranged 1–46 sessions with no clear dose moderator (Low 2021 — META-BACKED). Perceived pressure comes mainly from *consequences* (judgement, forfeits, rewards), not from making the task harder (Stoker 2017 — PRIMARY). The pre-competition block should schedule a few consequence-laden run-throughs; it does not need many.

7. **Stop changing the competition routine in the last days.** Three converging lines: reactivating a consolidated sequence and practising a *modified* version degrades the original (Walker 2003 — PRIMARY); overlearning past plateau "hyperstabilises" a skill against subsequent competing learning (Shibata 2017 — PRIMARY, perceptual task); and explicit late corrections are precisely the material that gets reinvested under pressure (§5). **PRIMARY on mechanisms; SPECULATIVE on the exact freeze window.**

8. **Load management is a separate axis from learning, and dance evidence is thin but pointed.** Dancers' injuries cluster after *sudden spikes* in weekly hours, typically in the final weeks before performances (Armando 2025 — META/scoping); a professional company logged ~6,700 AU/week with "few recovery periods" and all 16 dancers injured within a year (Jeffries 2020 — PRIMARY). The acute:chronic workload ratio itself is discredited as a causal tool (Impellizzeri 2020). Rule: track sRPE, cap week-on-week growth, and make the pre-competition block a *volume* taper — never a spike. **PRIMARY/META on the spike; CONTESTED on any specific ratio.**

9. **Let the dancer edit the proposed session; do not expect a learning gain from it.** Self-chosen practice order beat yoked control in small studies (Keetch & Lee 2007; Wu & Magill 2011 — PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED), but the field's meta-analysis finds the effect collapses to g = 0.02 in pre-registered experiments (McKay 2022 — META-BACKED). Autonomy is cheap and humane; it is not a mechanism. **Propose, let adjust, and use the edits as data.**

## 1. Order within a session

**Finding.** Three separable reasons justify "established → new/precision → variable/interleaved → whole routine" as the default order, and they are not equally strong. (a) *Warm-up / warm-up decrement* is a robust **performance** effect: after any rest the first attempts are worse until the "activity set" is re-instated. (b) *Fatigue* reliably degrades performance during practice; whether it degrades **learning** is contested, with the best modern study saying yes and the old literature saying no. (c) *Difficulty ordering* (easy-first vs hard-first) has been tested directly and does **not** produce the clean advantage textbooks imply. There is **no** primacy/recency literature for motor sessions worth citing.

**Evidence.**

- **Warm-up improves performance, and the effect is about readiness, not learning.** Fradkin, Zazryn & Smoliga (2010), *J Strength & Conditioning Research* 24(1):140–148 — **VERIFIED.** Systematic review with meta-analysis of 32 high-quality studies (only warm-ups that included activity beyond stretching): warm-up improved performance in **79% of the criteria examined**, with "little evidence to suggest that warming-up is detrimental." **META-BACKED.** All outcomes are acute performance; none is a retention test. I found **no** study that manipulates warm-up and measures motor *learning* — the question is simply unstudied. **(unverified absence, but searched.)**
- **Warm-up decrement and the activity-set hypothesis.** Adams (1961) named *warm-up decrement*: performance drops after a rest and recovers within a few trials of resuming the skill. Nacson & Schmidt (1971), *J Motor Behavior* 3(1):1–15 — **VERIFIED** (title/journal; pages unverified) — showed the decrement is a loss of a *generalised activity set* (arousal, attention to relevant feedback) rather than of the habit itself: an interpolated, task-similar activity before the test trials eliminated the decrement, whereas plain rest produced a large but quickly-recovered one. **PRIMARY.** Consequence for session design: the first reps after a break (start of session, after a water stop, after coach talk) are systematically *unrepresentative* — a bad place to score a card or to judge whether a change "took."
- **Fatigue and learning — the older literature: performance yes, learning no.** Alderman (1965), *Research Quarterly* 36:131–140 — **VERIFIED** — interpolated local fatigue impaired performance during practice of two motor tasks, but a retention test after full recovery showed **no difference** between fatigued and non-fatigued groups. Godwin & Schmidt (1971), *Research Quarterly* 42(4):374–382 — **VERIFIED** — a 2-minute arm-ergometer bout before each of 20 trials of a discrete arm task (n = 64 women) in a design framed by "a number of theoretical statements predict fatigue should be detrimental to learning, [yet] recent evidence has produced varied results." **PRIMARY.** These are the studies behind the textbook line that "fatigue affects performance but not learning" — small samples, simple tasks.
- **Fatigue and learning — the modern counter-evidence.** Branscheidt, Kassavetis, Anaya, Rogers, Huang, Lindquist & Celnik (2019), "Fatigue induces long-lasting detrimental changes in motor-skill learning," *eLife* 8:e40578 — **VERIFIED.** 121 participants across experiments. Fatigue induced by voluntary exertion to ~60% force decline *before* training on an isometric pinch-force skill. On **Day 2, without fatigue,** the fatigued group's learning rate was still reduced; at the end of Day 2 they had reached only **68%** of what controls reached at the end of Day 1, and needed roughly two more days to catch up. The deficit transferred to the *untrained, non-fatigued* hand (so it is central, not muscular), was **partly rescued by disruptive rTMS to motor cortex after Day 1** (i.e., a *maladaptive memory* formed under fatigue), and did **not** appear for a cognitive sequence task. Authors' conclusion: "a centrally-mediated, domain-specific deleterious effect of fatigue on motor-skill learning beyond impairment in execution." **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.**
- **The field's honest summary: inconsistent.** Ghafari Goushe et al. (2025), "Effects of experimentally induced fatigue on motor learning: a scoping review," bioRxiv preprint — **(unverified — preprint, not peer-reviewed).** 22 experimental studies: **59% negative** effect on learning (45% physical, 14% cognitive fatigue), **27% no effect** (all physical), 9% positive; the authors stress "considerable inconsistencies in how fatigue and motor learning were defined and measured." **CONTESTED.**
- **Easy-to-difficult ordering is not a free lunch.** Sanli & Lee (2014), "What roles do errors serve in motor skill learning? An examination of two theoretical predictions," *J Motor Behavior* 46(5):329–337 — **VERIFIED.** Two experiments contrasting easy→difficult vs difficult→easy progressions with retention, dual-task and transfer tests. Easy→difficult "did not consistently induce implicit learning processes and was not consistently beneficial to performance under a secondary-task load"; schema-theory predictions were not supported and reinvestment-theory predictions only partially. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (null-ish).** So the errorless→errorful *ordering* (as opposed to errorless *learning* per se, §4/§5) is weaker than its reputation.
- **Complex skills invert some simple-skill rules.** Wulf & Shea (2002), "Principles derived from the study of simple skills do not generalize to complex skill learning," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 9(2):185–211 — **VERIFIED.** Review concluding that conditions which *add* load (random practice, reduced feedback) help when processing demands are low, but "practice conditions that result in extremely high load should benefit from conditions that reduce the load to more manageable levels." **PRIMARY (review).** For ordering, this argues against front-loading a novice's session with several demanding new items.

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- Branscheidt's fatigue protocol (exertion to 60% force loss, then learning) is far more severe than a normal dance session's fatigue; the effect size at *realistic* fatigue is unknown. Treat the finding as "the risk is real and asymmetric," not as "any tiredness ruins learning."
- Warm-up decrement research is on simple tasks; the *duration* of the decrement in a 30-minute figure-based session is unmeasured. The activity-set account suggests it is short (a few reps) if the warm-up is task-similar.
- The only *learning-based* justification for "new before routine" in the literature is fatigue avoidance plus the marking finding (§9a: a full-out run-through is cognitively expensive). "Fresh attention = better encoding" is intuitive but I found no motor-learning study that isolates it. **(unverified.)**

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Open every session with 1–2 established cards** at low stakes (nothing scored, no new instruction) — this re-instates the activity set (Nacson & Schmidt) and is the task-similar warm-up Fradkin's data reward. **THEORY-DERIVED.**
- **Place cognitive-stage cards (new figures, technical changes) immediately after that opener**, before any card that produces meaningful fatigue. The asymmetry: if fatigue harms learning (Branscheidt), this ordering saves days; if it does not (Alderman), the ordering costs nothing. **CONTESTED evidence, THEORY-DERIVED rule.**
- **Interleaved/variable work (schema-building variants, mixed entries and exits) sits in the middle third**, once established cards are re-instated and the new card has had its blocked reps (§3 "block, then interleave").
- **Whole-routine / full-out / pressure run-throughs go last** — they are the fatiguing, stamina-relevant, performance-shaped work. Where the goal of the run-through is sequence/spacing rather than stamina, **mark it** (Warburton 2013, §9a) so it can sit later in the session without the fatigue cost.
- **Do not score a card on its first reps after any break.** If the app measures success, ignore the first 2–3 attempts after a pause. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

## 2. How many different skills per session, and the new-to-review ratio

**Finding.** The literature gives a clear answer to *what kind* of combination is dangerous (two competing memories learned in the same window) and **no** answer to *how many* items a session can hold. Music pedagogy adds that practice *quality* (error rate per trial) predicts retention far better than quantity. Dance cognition research describes *how* choreography is chunked and remembered but provides no dosing numbers. Overlearning research adds a twist: a skill drilled well past plateau not only resists interference but *blocks* new learning of a competing skill for a while.

**Evidence.**

- **Retroactive interference between two motor tasks — the classic.** Brashers-Krug, Shadmehr & Bizzi (1996), "Consolidation in human motor memory," *Nature* 382:252–255 — **VERIFIED.** Learning a second force-field *immediately* after the first disrupted retention of the first; a **4-hour** gap abolished the disruption. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** The *timing* side of this belongs to the spacing brief; the point here is what the two tasks were: **opposing** perturbations of the *same* arm movement. This is the archetype of a confusable pair. (Later force-field work — e.g., Caithness et al. 2004 — found the interference can persist even at 24 h, so the "4 hours is safe" number is itself **CONTESTED (unverified detail)**.)
- **Interference is specific to competing mappings.** Krakauer, Ghez & Ghilardi (2005), "Adaptation to visuomotor transformations: consolidation, interference, and forgetting," *J Neuroscience* 25(2):473–478 — **VERIFIED.** Adapting to a 30° cursor rotation showed savings (faster relearning) 24 h later; learning a **30° counter-rotation 5 minutes after** the original abolished those savings. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Lundbye-Jensen, Petersen, Rothwell & Nielsen (2011), "Interference in ballistic motor learning: specificity and role of sensory error signals," *PLoS ONE* 6(3):e17451 — **VERIFIED.** Interference with a ballistic task occurred **only if the competing task involved the same muscles and movement direction.** **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Cantarero, Tang, O'Malley, Salas & Celnik (2013), *J Neuroscience* 33(11):4634–4641 — **VERIFIED** — the degree of behavioural interference scales with occlusion of LTP-like plasticity in motor cortex: learning consumes a shared, finite plasticity resource that a second *similar* task then competes for. **PRIMARY.**
- **Two similar long sequences.** Panzer, Wilde & Shea (2006), "Learning of similar complex movement sequences: proactive and retroactive effects on learning," *J Motor Behavior* 38(1):60–70 — **VERIFIED.** Two similar 16-element arm sequences learned on consecutive days: strong **proactive facilitation** early in the second sequence's practice (the first helped start the second) that had vanished by the end of practice and at retention. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** A follow-up (Panzer & Shea 2008, *Human Movement Science*, "does practice insulate a sequence from interference?") examined whether more practice on the first sequence protects it — **(unverified detail).** Net: similar sequences help each other *initially* and compete *later* — the classic dance experience of two choreographies "bleeding" into each other.
- **Overlearning hyperstabilises — and blocks the next thing.** Shibata, Sasaki, Bang, Walsh, Machizawa, Tamaki, Chang & Watanabe (2017), "Overlearning hyperstabilizes a skill by rapidly making neurochemical processing inhibitory-dominant," *Nature Neuroscience* 20:470–475 — **VERIFIED.** Continuing to train a (visual perceptual) skill after performance had plateaued made it resistant to interference from a competing skill trained afterwards — and *disrupted* learning of that competing skill — with an MRS-measured shift from glutamate- to GABA-dominant processing. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (perceptual, not motor task).** Implication for a session: the *order* of an overlearned and a new confusable item matters; drilling A past plateau then immediately learning A′ is the worst combination for A′.
- **Complexity caps the number of demanding items.** Wulf & Shea (2002), above — high-load conditions call for load *reduction*. Carey, Moran & Rooney (2019), §9b — beginners show markedly higher attentional effort (pupil dilation) just to initiate a single 15-s phrase. **PRIMARY.**
- **Music: quality of trials, not quantity, predicts retention.** Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009), "It's not how much; it's how: Characteristics of practice behavior and retention of performance skills," *J Research in Music Education* 56(4):310–321 — **VERIFIED.** 17 advanced pianists practised a difficult 3-bar passage; retention ranking was **unrelated** to practice time, total trials or complete trials, and **related** to the percentage of trials played correctly and the number of *incorrect* trials. The best learners stopped, isolated the error, and fixed it before continuing. **PRIMARY (observational, n = 17).** Carter & Grahn (2016), "Optimizing music learning: Exploring how blocked and interleaved practice schedules affect advanced performance," *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1251 — **VERIFIED** (title/journal) — advanced clarinettists learning two passages under blocked vs interleaved schedules; interleaving improved next-day performance ratings **(effect-size detail unverified).** **PRIMARY.**
- **Dance cognition: structure, not dose.** Bläsing, Calvo-Merino, Cross, Jola, Honisch & Stevens (2012), "Neurocognitive control in dance perception and performance," *Acta Psychologica* 139:300–308 — **VERIFIED (title/journal; pages unverified).** Reviews how dancers' movement memory is more functionally chunked than non-dancers' and how phrase segmentation depends on expertise and music. It contains **no** data on the number of new phrases that can be absorbed per session or week. I found **no** study anywhere quantifying an intake rate of new figures/choreography for dancers. **(unverified absence, searched.)**
- **The periodization literature says "quantify skill load" but gives no threshold.** Farrow & Robertson (2017; §3 below) reconceptualise *Overload* for skill as the count and difficulty of attempts per session/week, to be monitored longitudinally — explicitly leaving the dose to be found empirically per athlete. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- Every interference study above used two tasks that were *deliberately* in conflict. No study asked "how many *unrelated* skills can be learned in one session" — the cap, if any, is a working-memory/attention question, not a consolidation one.
- Duke's finding is a correlation among 17 expert pianists on a 3-bar passage; it is a strong hint about *how* to run a practice block, not a law.
- The overlearning result is in vision, not motor cortex; its transfer to figure-drilling is **SPECULATIVE.**

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Guard against confusable pairs, not against count.** Define a "conflicts-with" relation between cards (mirror-image variants; the same figure in two timings/characterisations; two routines sharing an entry). Never put two cards from a conflict set into the *new/technical* slot of the same session; if one is established and one is new, the established one may appear only in the late *routine* slot, never adjacent to the new one. **PRIMARY on mechanism; SPECULATIVE on the mapping.**
- **Limit *cognitive-stage* cards, not total cards.** Default to **1 new card per session for novices, at most 2 for experienced dancers**, with the remainder of the session made of associative/autonomous-stage cards. This is a **SPECULATIVE** default derived from Wulf & Shea, Carey, and the interference specificity results; the app should expose it as a setting and log outcomes.
- **Prefer "few, clean" reps for the new card** (Duke): the app's rep target for a new card should be phrased as "N *correct* reps" with an "isolate and fix" prompt on error, not "N reps."
- **Weekly intake is unknowable from the literature** — make "new cards per week" a user-visible dial with a conservative default (e.g., 2–3) and let the board's own retention data (§ spacing brief) tune it. **SPECULATIVE.**

## 3. Periodization of skill, not fitness

**Finding.** Two frameworks import periodization into skill training. Both are candid that they are conceptual proposals, not tested programs. Their shared, defensible content: (i) skill load should be *quantified and monitored* like physical load; (ii) variability, complexity and representativeness should *rise* through a cycle; (iii) near competition, *stability and confidence* displace new learning; (iv) neglected skills decay (reversibility) and monotony harms (tedium), so a plan needs re-introduction and variety. Tapering research is entirely about conditioning and has nothing to say about skill.

**Evidence.**

- **Farrow & Robertson (2017), "Development of a skill acquisition periodisation framework for high-performance sport," *Sports Medicine* 47(6):1043–1054 — VERIFIED (author PDF read).** Takes the physical-training principles **S**pecificity, **P**rogression, **O**verload, **R**eversibility, **T**edium and re-conceptualises each for skill. *Specificity* → representative practice, monitored acutely and longitudinally. *Progression* → observable change in skill performance, with the caution that the contextual-interference effect "may lead both a coach and the athlete to mistake progression (or lack thereof)" because random practice looks worse (cf. §3 of the main brief), and that some sessions have goals other than progression ("the development of athlete confidence may be the priority which likely will require different practice demands"). *Overload* → skill load quantified from attempts, intensity and difficulty, importable from sRPE-style internal-load thinking. *Reversibility* → skills not practised decay; competition is "the ultimate transfer test"; and consolidation/"offline learning" during sleep or rest "may play an important role," so reversibility is not simply the absence of practice. They propose plotting neglected skills to trigger "scheduling of further practice of the neglected skills." *Tedium* → variety (contextual interference, variability, constraint manipulation) to avoid monotony, plus athlete *choice* — "an important caveat is that the choices made by the athletes need to be regulated relative to the principles detailed throughout this paper." The paper is a framework with worked-example figures, no intervention study. **THEORY-DERIVED.**
- **Otte, Millar & Klatt (2019), "Skill training periodization in 'specialist' sports coaching — an introduction of the 'PoST' framework for skill development," *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* 1:61 — VERIFIED (PMC full text read). Note: the journal is *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*, not *Frontiers in Psychology* as the research question assumed.** Three stages: **Coordination Training** (exploring coordination in a low-variability, low-complexity environment); **Skill Adaptability Training** with three sub-stages — *movement variability training* (within-skill variability, constraints varied but affordances stable), *complex training* (between-skill variability, several movement tasks), *team-based training* (game-representative); and **Performance Training**, "close to competition," where "skill development may not necessarily be the primary focus" and "perceived task complexity and game-representativeness stay well below" the maximum in order to emphasise "movement stability in order to build up confidence." The authors state the framework is theoretical and meant "to encourage future multi-disciplinary research," and provides no quantitative measure of representativeness. **THEORY-DERIVED.** (A goalkeeping case study applying it: Otte, Davids, Millar & Klatt 2020, *Int J Sports Science & Coaching* — **(unverified detail).**)
- **Tapering is conditioning-only.** Bosquet, Montpetit, Arvisais & Mujika (2007), "Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis," *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 39(8):1358–1365 — **VERIFIED.** 27 studies of competitive athletes with field performance outcomes: the most effective taper was **~2 weeks, training volume reduced exponentially by 41–60%, with intensity and frequency maintained.** All outcomes are physiological performance (swim/run/cycle times, strength). **Nothing on skill, technique or retention.** **META-BACKED for conditioning; irrelevant for learning.** The only skill-relevant reading is indirect: *volume* down, *intensity/specificity* up matches what PoST prescribes for the performance-training stage.
- **Dance-specific: the taper that actually happens is a spike.** See §5 — Armando et al. (2025) found injuries rising after sudden increases in weekly hours "during the final weeks leading up to performances."

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- Neither framework has been tested against an un-periodized control. Their value is organisational (a shared vocabulary for "what phase are we in and what should practice look like"), not demonstrated superiority. **CONTESTED by absence.**
- Both frameworks are written for team/open-skill sport; the dance analogues (representativeness = real floor, music, costume, being watched; between-skill variability = mixed figure sequences and different partners) are **SPECULATIVE** mappings.
- PoST's "performance training" stage explicitly *lowers* complexity — which conflicts with a naive reading of "make it harder as competition approaches." The reconciliation in both papers: *pressure and specificity* go up; *novelty and instability* go down.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Give the board a cycle phase** (Build / Adapt / Perform, mirroring PoST) and let it change the session template rather than the card pool: Build = blocked new material, low variability, more feedback; Adapt = variable and interleaved work, mixed sequences, fading feedback (§2 of the main brief); Perform = few or no new cards, stable versions of routines, representative conditions, pressure run-throughs, volume tapered. **THEORY-DERIVED.**
- **Quantify skill load per session** (attempts × difficulty, or simply session-RPE × minutes) so that Overload and Reversibility can be *seen*: cards not practised for a long time surface (this is the spacing brief's job to time; this brief's job is to say the periodization frameworks want it visible).
- **Vary the session shape across the week** (Tedium): do not generate the same ordering and the same variants every day.

## 4. The pre-competition window

**Finding.** In the final days, the evidence favours *stabilising* what will be performed and *rehearsing the conditions* of performance, and disfavours *introducing changes*. Three mechanisms converge on "freeze the routine": reconsolidation (a reactivated memory can be overwritten by practising a variant), overlearning (a well-drilled skill resists — and is disrupted by — competing learning), and reinvestment (late explicit corrections are what an anxious dancer will consciously re-control; §5 of the main brief). Pressure training has a solid meta-analytic effect and no established dose. Specificity-of-practice says the feedback conditions of practice should match those of the competition.

**Evidence.**

- **Reconsolidation: modifying a consolidated sequence can degrade it.** Walker, Brakefield, Hobson & Stickgold (2003), "Dissociable stages of human memory consolidation and reconsolidation," *Nature* 425:616–620 — **VERIFIED (title/journal; pages from memory).** A finger-tapping sequence learned on Day 1 was stable by Day 2; but *briefly rehearsing it* on Day 2 and then learning a *competing* sequence degraded Day-3 performance of the original — reactivation re-opened the memory to interference. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (simple task).** For dance: touching the competition routine and then drilling a *modified* version of a figure in it is exactly the reactivate-then-compete pattern.
- **Overlearning stabilises against later competition.** Shibata et al. (2017), §2 above. Continued training after plateau made the skill "resilient against, but disrupt[ive of], subsequent new learning." **PRIMARY.** Driskell, Willis & Copper (1992), "Effect of overlearning on retention," *J Applied Psychology* 77(5):615–622 — **(unverified pages)** — meta-analysis: overlearning improves retention, more for cognitive than physical tasks, with decay over time; covered in a companion brief, cited here only to note that overlearning the *routine itself* in the final block is doubly justified (retention + stabilisation).
- **Reinvestment.** Masters (1992) and Beilock & Carr (2001) — see §5 of the main brief. The mechanism implies that every explicit rule added in the final days is a new candidate for conscious re-control under judging.
- **Pressure training — the meta-analysis.** Low, Sandercock, Freeman, Winter, Butt & Maynard (2021), "Pressure training for performance domains: A meta-analysis," *Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology* 10(1):149–163 — **VERIFIED (repository PDF read).** 14 studies (10 sport, 4 law enforcement), n = 394, mean sample 28. Effect on high-pressure post-tests vs control: **g = 0.85 [0.37, 1.34]; g = 0.67 [0.41, 0.94] after removing one outlier.** Doses ranged **1 to 46 sessions**; subgroup analyses found **no clear moderators** (domain, dose, task type, experience), though long-dose interventions had the smallest and least precise estimate (g = 0.42 [−0.65, 1.50], k = 3). "PT can benefit both novice and experienced participants on open and closed tasks." The most common pressure manipulations were **judgement (k = 8), rewards (k = 6), forfeits (k = 4).** The authors call for research on minimum and maximum doses "in case longer doses diminish perceived pressure." **META-BACKED (small k, wide CIs).**
- **What actually creates pressure.** Stoker, Maynard, Butt, Hays, Lindsay & Norenberg (2017), "The effect of manipulating training demands and consequences on experiences of pressure in elite netball," *J Applied Sport Psychology* 29(4):434–448 — **VERIFIED via Low et al.'s reference list.** Randomised within-subject: control / consequences / demands / demands + consequences. Self-reported pressure was significantly higher with **consequences** (and demands + consequences) but **not with demands alone.** **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** Making the task harder is not pressure training; attaching a judged outcome is.
- **Specificity of practice.** Proteau, Marteniuk & Lévesque (1992), "A sensorimotor basis for motor learning: Evidence indicating specificity of practice," *Quarterly J Experimental Psychology A* 44(3):557–575 — **VERIFIED (title/journal; pages unverified).** Removing or adding vision at test degraded aiming performance, and the cost *increased* with the amount of practice: the learner builds a representation specific to the feedback available during practice. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED.** For dance this is the mirror/video argument of §9e and §2 of the main brief, sharpened: the more you practise with the mirror, the more the mirror-less floor costs.

**Caveats / boundary conditions.**
- The reconsolidation and overlearning results are from finger-tapping and visual tasks; the *size* of the freeze window for a 90-second routine is unknown. Coaches' folk rule ("no new material in the last week") is consistent with the mechanisms but not derived from them. **SPECULATIVE window.**
- Low et al.'s pooled effect sits on 14 heterogeneous studies with a mean n of 28; the CI is wide and the outlier sensitivity is real. Treat "pressure training helps" as robust and "by g = 0.67" as soft.
- Pressure exposure that is too frequent may stop feeling like pressure (Low et al.'s own concern), and excessive consequences can harm confidence — the PoST "confidence" priority pulls the other way.

**Coaching / app translation.**
- **Freeze mode:** for a configurable window (default 7 days) before a listed competition, the builder (a) admits no cognitive-stage cards, (b) admits no "change" tasks on cards that appear in the competition routines, and (c) runs each competition routine whole at least once per session. **PRIMARY mechanisms, SPECULATIVE window.**
- **Pressure run-throughs are consequence-based, few, and scheduled:** 2–4 sessions in the last two weeks containing one run-through with a real consequence (filmed and shown to the coach; scored against a stated target; a forfeit). Not every session, not every run. **META-BACKED on effect; SPECULATIVE on dose.**
- **Representative conditions rise across the window:** no mirror, competition music and tempo, costume, floor traffic (other couples), being watched. **PRIMARY (specificity); §5/§7 of main brief.**
- **Feedback and video fade to near zero** (§2 of the main brief) — the same specificity logic.

## 5. Session load and injury in dance (load management, not learning)

**Finding.** Dance-medicine research is small, mostly single-company, and does not yet support a numeric load threshold. What it does show: session-RPE is a valid load measure in dance; professional and pre-professional dancers carry high, rarely-interrupted loads; overuse symptoms dominate; and injuries cluster after *sudden increases* in weekly hours, typically before performances. The acute:chronic workload ratio, popular in team sport, has been dismantled as a causal tool. This section is here **only** to constrain session-building (rest days, growth caps, no pre-competition spike).

**Evidence.**
- Jeffries, Wallace & Coutts (2017), "Quantifying training loads in contemporary dance," *Int J Sports Physiology & Performance* 12(6):796–802 — **VERIFIED.** 16 elite amateur contemporary dancers, 49 days, heart rate + accelerometry + sRPE: sRPE was a valid measure of dance training intensity/load. **PRIMARY.**
- Jeffries, Wallace, Coutts, Cohen, McCall & Impellizzeri (2020), "Injury, illness, and training load in a professional contemporary dance company: a prospective study," *J Athletic Training* 55(9) — **VERIFIED (PMC full text read).** 16 professionals, 14,689 participation hours over a year. Weekly load 6,685 ± 1,605 AU. Medical-attention injuries **4.6/1000 h**, time-loss **1.4/1000 h**; **all 16 dancers were injured** within the year. The load–injury relation *reversed sign* depending on whether load percentiles were group- or individual-based, so the authors refuse a causal claim, but note "few recovery periods occurred within and between macrocycles" and recommend "including recovery days and short periods of reduced load." **PRIMARY (observational).**
- Boeding, Visser, Meuffels & de Vos (2019), "Is training load associated with symptoms of overuse injury in dancers? A prospective observational study," *J Dance Medicine & Science* 23(1):11–18 — **VERIFIED.** 21 professional contemporary dancers, 7 weeks: no association between load and pain *severity*, but dancers with **no** overuse symptoms had significantly lower load (p = 0.02); 67% of recorded time was danced with pain. **PRIMARY (observational).**
- Armando, Kurapati, Voulo & Gallo (2025), "Influence of training load on the risk of injuries in preprofessional contemporary dancers: a scoping review," *Orthopaedic J Sports Medicine* — **VERIFIED (Europe PMC abstract).** 9 studies; overuse injuries 20–80% of all injuries; "a sudden spike in training hours during the final weeks leading up to performances resulted in a higher incidence of injuries" (cubic-spline model, p = 0.037); conclusion: "erratic training schedules — particularly rapid increases in weekly training hours — significantly contribute to heightened injury rates." **META-BACKED (scoping review; small evidence base).**
- Impellizzeri, Tenan, Kempton, Novak & Coutts (2020), "Acute:chronic workload ratio: conceptual issues and fundamental pitfalls," *Int J Sports Physiology & Performance* 15(6):907–913 — **VERIFIED (title/journal).** Argues the ratio has no demonstrated causal validity, poor statistical properties, and that manipulating it to reduce injury "remains conjecture." **PRIMARY (methodological critique).** **CONTESTED** in the wider field, but the burden has shifted to ACWR's defenders.

**Caveats.** Every dance study above is contemporary/ballet, single-cohort, and observational; there is no DanceSport load study that I could find. **(unverified absence.)** No number in this section is a threshold.

**App translation (load management only).**
- **Record sRPE × minutes per session** (a 0–10 exertion rating on session close). It is the one validated dance load measure. **PRIMARY.**
- **Cap week-on-week growth in total minutes or AU** (a conservative default such as +10–15%) and **warn on spikes**, especially in the two weeks before a competition — the pattern the dance data most consistently indict. **META-BACKED on the pattern; SPECULATIVE on the number.**
- **Protect at least one full rest day per week** and flag runs of consecutive high-load days — the concrete recommendation of the only year-long professional cohort. **PRIMARY (recommendation, not trial).**
- **Do not compute or display an ACWR.** **CONTESTED/discredited.**

## 6. Autonomy and self-selected session composition

**Finding.** Small experiments found that learners who *chose their own practice order* learned better than yoked controls, and that they spontaneously chose sensible progressions (blocked early, more switching later). The field-wide meta-analysis, however, shows the self-controlled-learning literature is underpowered and publication-biased, with the effect near zero in pre-registered studies. The motivational mechanism proposed for the effect was never measured in most studies. Net: offering choice is harmless and pleasant; expecting it to *produce* learning is not supported.

**Evidence.**
- Keetch & Lee (2007), "The effect of self-regulated and experimenter-imposed practice schedules on motor learning for tasks of varying difficulty," *Research Quarterly for Exercise & Sport* 78(5):476–486 — **VERIFIED.** Cursor-path task, four easy or four hard patterns, groups: blocked / random / self-regulated / yoked. Self-regulation gave no acquisition advantage but "the most improved performance in retention, irrespective of task difficulty." **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (small n).**
- Wu & Magill (2011), "Allowing learners to choose: Self-controlled practice schedules for learning multiple movement patterns," *Research Quarterly for Exercise & Sport* 82(3):449–457 — **VERIFIED (title/journal; pages unverified).** 30 participants learned three relative-timing patterns of a keystroke sequence; the self-control group chose which pattern to practise before each of 90 trials. On a 24-h transfer test they had lower relative-timing, absolute and total error than yoked controls. The literature cites it for the observation that learners progressed from low to higher contextual interference on their own. **PRIMARY/RCT-BACKED (n = 30).** (The exact title "Temporal changes in learners' self-regulated practice schedule" in the research question could not be located; the paper above is Wu & Magill's 2011 RQES self-controlled-schedule study.)
- Sanli, Patterson, Bray & Lee (2013), "Understanding self-controlled motor learning protocols through the self-determination theory," *Frontiers in Psychology* 3:611 — **VERIFIED.** Review of 26 empirical studies: the assumed motivational mechanism (autonomy → competence/motivation → learning) had been "assumed rather than quantified." **PRIMARY (review).**
- McKay, Yantha, Hussien, Carter & Ste-Marie (2022), "Meta-analytic findings in the self-controlled motor learning literature: Underpowered, biased, and lacking evidential value," *Meta-Psychology* 6, MP.2021.2803 — **VERIFIED.** Naive random-effects estimate **g = 0.44** (k = 52, N = 2061, 95% CI [0.31, 0.56]); among **pre-registered** experiments **g = 0.02 [−0.17, 0.21]**; median power low, evidence of bias. **META-BACKED.** Corroborated by the pre-registered N = 228 null (St. Germain et al. 2022) already in §2 of the main brief, and by Parma et al. 2024 on OPTIMAL's motivational claims (§1).
- Farrow & Robertson (2017) independently caution that athlete choices "need to be regulated relative to the principles" of the plan. **THEORY-DERIVED.**

**Caveats.** Keetch & Lee and Wu & Magill are exactly the kind of small, unregistered studies McKay et al. show to be over-optimistic; they are not refuted individually, but the prior for a real effect is low. The one durable observation is *behavioural*: given choice, learners tend to choose a blocked-then-random progression and to reduce feedback over time (§2 of the main brief, Ste-Marie 2013) — a hint about a sensible default, not a learning mechanism.

**App translation.**
- **Propose, don't impose — and don't sell it as pedagogy.** The builder generates a session; the dancer can reorder, drop, swap, add, and mark a card "not today" in one gesture. The justification is *fit to the day* (soreness, partner availability, floor, mood) and user retention, not motor learning. **META-BACKED (null on learning).**
- **Constrain the edits by the plan's hard rules** (Farrow & Robertson): the conflict-set guard (§2), the freeze window (§4) and the load caps (§5) survive editing, with an explicit override.
- **Learn from the edits.** If a dancer consistently moves a card earlier, drops a category, or shortens the routine block, that is preference data the builder should absorb — and the *behavioural* self-scheduling literature suggests those choices will tend toward sensible progressions.

## 7. Design implications for the app — session-assembly rules

Each rule carries the evidence label of its *weakest* load-bearing link. "Slot" refers to a position in the generated session.

### 7.1 Ordering (from §1)

1. **Opener slot: 1–2 established (autonomous-stage) cards, unscored.** Re-instates the activity set; absorbs warm-up decrement; task-similar warm-up. *THEORY-DERIVED (Nacson & Schmidt 1971; Fradkin 2010).*
2. **New/technical slot immediately after the opener.** Cognitive-stage cards and any card flagged "changing something" go here, before fatigue accumulates. *CONTESTED evidence (Branscheidt 2019 vs Alderman 1965), THEORY-DERIVED rule; asymmetric downside justifies the default.*
3. **Variable/interleaved slot in the middle.** Associative-stage cards, schema variants (tempo, size, entry/exit), mixed sequences. *§3 of main brief; block-then-interleave.*
4. **Routine slot last.** Whole routines, full-out, stamina, pressure run-throughs. Offer "mark it" as the default when the goal is sequence/spacing rather than stamina. *PRIMARY (Warburton 2013) for marking; THEORY-DERIVED for placement.*
5. **Ignore the first 2–3 attempts after any pause when scoring a card.** *THEORY-DERIVED (warm-up decrement).*

### 7.2 Count and mix (from §2)

6. **Cap cognitive-stage cards per session: default 1 (novice) / 2 (experienced).** Expose as a setting. *SPECULATIVE, derived from Wulf & Shea 2002, Carey 2019, interference specificity.*
7. **No hard cap on total distinct cards**; the practical limit is the session's time budget and the load cap. *PRIMARY on the interference mechanism being similarity-specific.*
8. **Conflict sets.** Cards can declare "conflicts-with" (mirror variants, same figure with different timing/character, routines sharing material). Rule: at most one card of a conflict set in the new/technical slot per session; an established member of the set may appear only in the routine slot and never adjacent to the new one; never drill an established member past plateau *immediately before* its new sibling. *PRIMARY (Brashers-Krug 1996; Krakauer 2005; Lundbye-Jensen 2011; Shibata 2017); SPECULATIVE mapping to figures.*
9. **New:review ratio is a dial, not a rule.** Default ≈ 20:80 by time in the Build phase; ≈ 10:90 in Adapt; 0:100 in Perform. *SPECULATIVE — no intake-rate evidence exists.*
10. **New-card rep target is "N correct," with an isolate-and-fix prompt on error**, not "N reps." *PRIMARY (Duke 2009, small observational).*

### 7.3 Phase-dependent variants (from §3)

11. **Board-level phase: Build / Adapt / Perform.** The phase changes the session *template*, not the card pool:
    - *Build:* rules 1–10 as above; more feedback; variants low.
    - *Adapt:* variable slot grows; interleaving and mixed sequences; feedback fades; representative conditions begin (no mirror on some cards).
    - *Perform:* see 7.4.
    *THEORY-DERIVED (Farrow & Robertson 2017; Otte et al. 2019).*
12. **Reversibility surfacing.** Cards untouched for a long time are made visible to the builder (the spacing brief decides *when*; this brief says the periodization frameworks want it visible and re-introduced deliberately). *THEORY-DERIVED.*
13. **Tedium guard.** The builder varies ordering within slots and the choice of variants day to day; identical consecutive sessions are avoided. *THEORY-DERIVED.*

### 7.4 Pre-competition mode (from §4)

14. **Freeze window, default 7 days before a competition date:** no cognitive-stage cards; no "change" tasks on cards contained in a competition routine; every competition routine run whole at least once per session; feedback/video near zero. *PRIMARY mechanisms (Walker 2003; Shibata 2017; Masters 1992); SPECULATIVE window.*
15. **Pressure run-throughs: consequence-based, 2–4 in the final two weeks.** A run-through counts as pressure only if it carries a judged consequence (filmed-and-shown, scored against a stated target, forfeit). *META-BACKED effect (Low 2021); PRIMARY on consequences > demands (Stoker 2017); SPECULATIVE dose.*
16. **Representativeness ramp:** mirror off, competition music/tempo, costume, floor traffic, being watched — rising across the window. *PRIMARY (Proteau 1992; §5/§7 of main brief).*
17. **Volume taper, never a spike:** total minutes/AU in the freeze window ≤ the preceding weeks; warn if the plan rises. *META-BACKED on the injury pattern (Armando 2025); Bosquet 2007 is conditioning-only and cited only for the direction.*

### 7.5 Load (from §5)

18. **Collect sRPE (0–10) at session close; store AU = sRPE × minutes.** *PRIMARY (Jeffries 2017).*
19. **Warn on week-on-week growth above a default +10–15% and on any spike before a competition.** *META-BACKED pattern; SPECULATIVE threshold.*
20. **Protect ≥ 1 rest day per week; flag ≥ 3 consecutive high-load days.** *PRIMARY recommendation (Jeffries 2020), not a trial.*
21. **Do not display an acute:chronic ratio.** *CONTESTED/discredited (Impellizzeri 2020).*

### 7.6 Self-adjustment (from §6)

22. **Propose, then let the dancer reorder / drop / swap / add / "not today" in one gesture.** Justified by fit-to-the-day and usability, *not* by a learning claim. *META-BACKED null on learning (McKay 2022).*
23. **Hard rules survive edits with explicit override**: conflict sets, freeze window, load caps. *THEORY-DERIVED (Farrow & Robertson on regulated choice).*
24. **Treat edits as preference data** and let the builder adapt its defaults per dancer. *SPECULATIVE; consistent with the behavioural self-scheduling observations.*
